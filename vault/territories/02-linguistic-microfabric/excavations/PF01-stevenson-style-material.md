# PF01 — Robert Louis Stevenson: what prose is made from

**Primary:** Robert Louis Stevenson, *On Some Technical Elements of Style in Literature* (1885; collected in *Essays in the Art of Writing*), public-domain primary text https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/492/pg492-images.html (printed 1905 ed. pp. 3–46).

**Reading status:** direct close reading of the essay's four numbered divisions. The primary text makes explicit technical claims; the analyst must not convert those claims into universal demonstrated laws.

## What Stevenson actually says

**1. Word choice.** Words familiar from ordinary use can feel changed by new contexts, and can bring historical/literary associations; he cautions that modern, excessively ready-made expressions lose freshness. He specifically names and compares modes of diction rather than ranking prose solely by story significance.

**2. The web / relation of clauses.** Stevenson discusses meaning entwined with patterns of phrases: suspensions, expectations, reversals of normal word order, and resolution. He is explicit that the sonic pattern and the logic of the sentence must fit one another, and that empty padding merely to satisfy rhythm is cheating his ideal. This is **a period-specific aesthetic statement by Stevenson**.

**3. Rhythm of phrases.** He treats phrases as breath/stress groups of variable length: accented/unaccented, long/short. His contrast is between changing prose rhythms and the danger of allowing a succession of lines to fall into accidental verse metre. He remarks that listening matters and that formal rules cannot fully specify pleasure.

**4. Contents of a phrase.** Stevenson asks the reader to track alliteration, assonance, consonant recurrence/variation, and even **eye-rhymes or visually similar letters where pronunciations differ**. That last caution means visual letter repetition and audible phonemic repetition must be separated.

## Precisely attested scales

Word-level contextual associations; phrase-internal relations of sound and logic; sentence-level order and delayed completion; cross-phrase rhythm; patterned letter/sound recurrence across a larger passage. In one discussion he moves from local phoneme/letter relations into the identity of a larger pattern without implying that the artist consciously counted every occurrence.

## Grounded example to return to

The essay contrasts rhetorical ordering with meaningful progression by analysing how expectation may be raised and then satisfied or deliberately diverted. Stevenson invites comparisons in Shakespeare: Rumour's Prologue versus Falstaff's praise of sherris in *Henry IV, Part II*. This is a **named comparison target**; those dramatic passages were not independently examined in this tranche.

## Limit and counterpressure

Stevenson's distinction between beautiful prose and accidental blank verse is not a measurement; another creative world might want conspicuous regularity. He also makes broad historical generalisations about English and French prosody that require linguistic scrutiny. Do not treat cultural hierarchies in an 1885 essay as scientifically verified.
