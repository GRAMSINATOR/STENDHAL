# Style profile — The Atlantic feature / essay

## Engine

**A particular experience or contradiction expands into a larger idea.**

The Atlantic-like mode tolerates more authored movement than straight news: scene, curiosity, conversational correction, conceptual escalation and lexical play can coexist.

## Vector

- **Entry point:** scene, odd fact, contradiction, personal encounter, institutional decision.
- **Sequencing:** particular → curiosity/tension → explanation → conceptual claim → test/complication → wider meaning.
- **Compression:** medium.
- **Scene allowance:** medium to high.
- **Authorial presence:** visible but controlled.
- **Judgement:** analytical and essayistic.
- **Lexical markedness:** medium.
- **Humour / wit:** occasional, embedded in observation.
- **Sentence movement:** wide range; colloquial turns can sit beside long analytical sentences.
- **Evidence cadence:** looser than hard news but still explicit where claims require it.
- **Ending:** conceptual expansion, image return, or a sharpened contradiction.

## How to compose it

Look for a hinge:
- an event that appears self-contradictory;
- an odd object that reveals an institution;
- a personal experience that opens onto a general system;
- a familiar category that stops fitting.

Allow the narrator to admit uncertainty or correct an initial assumption when that movement is real.

## Language

A few strange, exact details can carry humour or attitude. Surround them with ordinary prose.

Do not confuse authorial presence with constant flourish.

## Failure mode

Bad imitation becomes:
- over-written;
- self-conscious;
- metaphor-rich but evidence-poor;
- a personal essay that forgets the subject.

If every paragraph contains a clever sentence, cut half of them.
