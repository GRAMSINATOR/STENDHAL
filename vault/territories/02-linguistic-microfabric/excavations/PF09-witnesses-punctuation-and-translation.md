# PF09 — A comma may belong to an edition, not the author: two textual audits

**Question:** How confident can a close reader be when attributing a microscopic mark to the writer? These are *witness differences*, not evidence of actual authorial revision.

## Austen: original 1816 printing versus Project Gutenberg

- **1816 first edition Vol. I p. 1:** validated textual transcription and facsimile page: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Austen_-_Emma,_vol._I,_1816.djvu/11
- **Project Gutenberg #158:** https://www.gutenberg.org/files/158/158-h/158-h.htm

The **first** sentence's semicolon after the initial appraisal appears in both. But the **second** sentence diverges twice:

| Location | 1816 witness | Gutenberg witness |
|---|---|---|
| After description of Emma's father | **father, and had** | **father; and had** |
| After remembrance of the mother's caresses | **caresses, and her place** | **caresses; and her place** |

The punctuational difference is visible and reproducible from these sources. It is *not yet known* whether the Gutenberg reading derives from a particular later edition, textual normalisation or another intervention.

**Critical restriction:** we cannot call the semicolon in Gutenberg 'Austen's deliberately controlled pause' at these two locations while ignoring the documented comma of the 1816 printed page.

**Further obstacle:** Austen's novel manuscripts, including *Emma*, are not extant. The scholarly digital manuscript edition states this directly: https://janeausten.ac.uk/edition/intro.html . We may compare printed witnesses, but cannot use a surviving original draft of *Emma* to resolve authorial intention.

## Flaubert: French witnesses of a syntactic pause

- **1885 Quantin edited printing, transcribed from images:** https://dev.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/48359/pg48359-images.html
- **French Gutenberg #14155:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/14155/pg14155.html

Early in Chapter I:
- The 1885 text reads **se leva, comme surpris**.
- The alternative French electronic text reads **se leva comme surpris**.

Capitalisation of *étude/proviseur* likewise differs. The added or omitted comma may change how readily one reads the comparison as parenthetical, but the distinction must not be attributed to Flaubert's personally intended prosodic pause without further editorial evidence.

## Translation as a second transformation of the material

**French:** *un nouveau habillé en bourgeois*

**Eleanor Marx-Aveling:** *a “new fellow,” not wearing the school uniform*

Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/files/2413/2413-h/2413-h.htm

The translator chooses to make the uniform contrast explicit, whereas the French identifies clothing through *bourgeois*. This is an observed contrast in expression, not grounds to call the translation wrong: the scene and context make the interpretation plausible. The substantivised French adjective and the English nominal phrase have different grammatical textures.

**French:** *Nous étions* / **English:** *We were*. Their referential positions are approximately aligned, but later narrator effects and rhythms must be examined in each language, not assumed identical.

## Research rule, warranted by the witnesses

Record the **specific edition, source language, translation, facsimile availability and text editorialisation** for every micro-claim. Separate:
1. What this witness prints.
2. How another witness differs.
3. Whether a historical author actually selected or revised the mark.
4. What a reader could plausibly experience from each variant.

A source-first claim at punctuation scale is invalid if it cannot identify the witness.
