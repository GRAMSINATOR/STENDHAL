# Region 05 — science writing: sentence placement, reader burden and historical convention

**Status:** primary journal article plus research abstracts/publisher records examined. No claim that these sources all disagree with each other or define a universal scientific style.

## Direct source 1 — Gopen & Swan 1990

George D. Gopen and Judith A. Swan, *The Science of Scientific Writing*, *American Scientist* 78 (1990), 550–558. Journal PDF: https://www.popecol.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/Gopen-Swan-1990.pdf ; alternative teaching transcription: https://courses.ems.psu.edu/styleforstudents/print/c10_p6.html .

The authors begin with a technically grammatical, field-specific scientific paragraph that remains difficult to comprehend. Their explanation concerns reader *expectations* about which unit is the grammatical actor and where a sentence provides familiar contextual perspective versus new emphasis.

Their terms **topic position** and **stress position** describe ends of a sentence as potential places for context versus closure/emphasis. Their actual examples recommend rearranging rather than merely shortening complex technical statements.

**Source status:** C2 original article text, introductory example and conceptual sections. Still need exact pre/post specimen and technical expert review before using it as a "fixer" for real science papers.

**Important:** these authors do not establish that every sentence literally has one function, or that actual readers always obey an invariant stress rule. The goal is reader comprehension in specialised exposition.

## Source 2 — Helen Sword, academic stylistic diversity

Helen Sword, *Stylish Academic Writing* (2012), author-led description and table of contents: https://www.helensword.com/stylish-academic-writing .

Sword reports analysing **more than a thousand peer-reviewed articles** across disciplines and observing a difference between stated academic preferences and the wordy, impersonal prose that many scholars produce. Her chapters separately examine voice, smart sentencing, openings, jargon, structure and creative choices.

**Source status:** C1 publisher/author account of empirical corpus, not direct replicated article statistics or individual before/after cases.

What this adds is an expert counterpressure against treating elaborate technical prose as an immovable requirement of all scholarly disciplines; it does **not** show that poetic cadence must be added to experimental sections.

## Source 3 — IMRAD is a historical practice, not natural law

Luciana B. Sollaci and Mauricio G. Pereira (2004), *The introduction, methods, results, and discussion (IMRAD) structure: a fifty-year survey*, *Journal of the Medical Library Association*. Open full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC442179/ .

The authors sampled original medical articles across four journals (BMJ, JAMA, Lancet, NEJM) over 1935–1985. The abstract states that IMRAD's uptake began in the 1940s, exceeded 80% in those journals in the 1970s and became the sampled default in the 1980s. This is an empirical claim about historical journal structure, **not** a result establishing its optimum epistemic quality.

**Source status:** C2 article and method/abstract; sample-specific proportions not extrapolated to all of science.

## Source 4 — Explicit anti-genre-homogenisation

Scientific communication can be constrained by evidential traceability (data, inference, reproducibility), yet precise prose remains a creative/linguistic problem. The same syntactic shift may increase readability but accidentally alter causal attribution. A written "story" or strong rhetorical arc might obscure uncertainty in an otherwise accurate analysis. These are **research hazards**, not assertions that Gopen, Sword or IMRAD researchers hold incompatible theses.
