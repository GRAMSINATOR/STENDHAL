# External research: phonological form, lexical neighbourhoods and meaning

**Status:** 2026-10-12, bibliographic/abstract-to-targeted-read entry points. This is **evidence that material sound can carry information for human language users**, not evidence that any particular LLM is missing that information or that literary quality can be numerically ranked.

## 01 — Sound symbolism / lexical iconic-systematicity

**David M. Sidhu**, "Sound Symbolism in the Lexicon: A Review of Iconic-Systematicity," *Language and Linguistics Compass* 19(1), 2025 (online December 2024), DOI https://doi.org/10.1111/lnc3.70006 .

**Source claim:** Review describes statistical regularities linking sounds (phonemes) and features including shape, size, texture, affective valence and arousal. It distinguishes *iconicity*—similarity of form to meaning—from *systematicity*—distributional patterning across a lexicon. Its examples include common sound-shape assignments such as maluma and takete, but the relationships are probabilistic, culturally situated, and debated in scope.

**What not to infer:** the phoneme /m/ always feels soft, or /k/ always feels harsh; a statistical relationship in a lexicon determines the emotional effect of one sentence; an LLM necessarily lacks such relationships.

**Research question:** which sound–meaning associations are robust in the speakers, languages and contexts relevant to a passage? Can an apparent effect survive controls for word frequency, existing lexical meanings and semantic context?

## 02 — Phonological iconicity beyond onomatopoeia

**Article:** "Phonological iconicity" (2014), open research review in *Frontiers in Psychology*, accessible at https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3921575/ .

**Source claim:** Reviews phonological links with onomatopoeia, ideophones, phonaesthemes, shape-sound mapping and sound–affect correspondences in multiple languages; contests the oversimplification that linguistic form and meaning have **no** nonarbitrary links.

**Boundary:** many observations are distributional or arise in restricted lexical classes; they are not a predictive creative-writing grammar.

**Research question:** how to distinguish conventional morphology, productive phonological patterns, accidentally similar spelling and sound-affect hypotheses in actual crafted sentences?

## 03 — Phonological neighbourhood effects in ordinary word processing

**Research review:** "Phonological Neighborhood Effects in Spoken Word Perception and Production," *Annual Review of Linguistics*, 2016, DOI https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-linguistics-030514-124832 .

**Source claim:** Spoken-word recognition and production involve activation and competition among similar-sounding lexical forms. Speed and accuracy depend partly on neighbourhood density and word frequency. This is **not an artistic-craft study**; it shows that the sound-related neighbourhood of a word matters to lexical cognition.

**Research question:** can a writer exploit or disrupt expectations carried by similar-sounding words? This is a *candidate question*, not an implication established by the spoken-word laboratory experiments.

## 04 — Computational auditory iconicity

**Article:** "Cracking arbitrariness: A data-driven study of auditory iconicity in spoken English," *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*, DOI https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-024-02630-0 .

**Source claim:** Uses computational analysis of auditory English vocabulary to find sound–referent resemblance exceeding chance, including when overtly onomatopoeic terms are excluded. The method is specialised and examines a particular semantic domain.

**Not established:** that the learned compact vectors constitute a universal representation of prose sound or human emotional responses; that an expressive writing engine requires embedding/vector training.

## How this relates to STENDHAL's central hypothesis

A possible under-indexing issue is not merely that a model sometimes miscounts syllables. It might underweight **sound relationships that human language users can recruit for association, memory, expectation, affect and lexical interpretation**. These may be subtle, entangled, or incongruent with literal semantic content.

Conversely, some of these relations may already be internally learned or available in an LLM; establishing their active use requires empirical testing rather than philosophical assertions.

Keep independent:
- **Human lexical science**: evidence about forms, associations, processing and populations.
- **LLM representation research**: evidence from probes and tokenisation conditions.
- **Craft close reading**: what accomplished writers and critics actually notice in passages.
- **Reader/listener evaluation**: experienced effects of real sentences.

The *same* word/sound relationship can be observed under each method without making the methods interchangeable.

## Next

Compare each source's tasks and effect sizes (not only abstract summaries). Look for null results, cross-linguistic contradictions, and studies separating phonetic similarity from orthographic cues. Do not convert a single published correlation into an expressive rule.
