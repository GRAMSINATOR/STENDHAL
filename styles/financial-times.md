# Style profile — Financial Times

## Engine

**Argument carried by facts, incentives, institutions and consequences.**

The FT-like mode is strongest when the story has money, power, organizational behaviour, policy, markets or professional incentives underneath it.

## Vector

- **Entry point:** material fact, decision, contradiction, market move, institutional oddity.
- **Sequencing:** establish what matters quickly, then follow incentives and consequences.
- **Compression:** high.
- **Scene allowance:** low to medium; scenes must reveal business or institutional mechanics.
- **Authorial presence:** restrained.
- **Judgement:** analytical; earned through mechanism, comparison and consequence.
- **Lexical markedness:** low, with occasional exact or dry surprise.
- **Humour / wit:** dry and sparse.
- **Sentence movement:** economical declaratives plus occasional longer explanatory sentences.
- **Evidence cadence:** frequent but unobtrusive.
- **Ending:** consequence, incentive, unresolved trade-off, or one dry return.

## How to compose it

Ask:
- Who bears the cost?
- Who captures the benefit?
- What incentive does the rule create?
- What changed in the balance sheet, market structure or institution?
- What second-order consequence is being missed?

Prefer specific commercial objects:
prices, contracts, margins, headcount, debt, regulation, customers, capacity, deadlines.

## Language

Most prose should be plain and controlled. Let one dry phrase register because the surrounding text does not compete with it.

Do not "make it FT" by adding British understatement everywhere.

## Typical shape

fact/decision → why it matters → mechanism/incentive → counter-pressure → consequence → restrained judgement

## Failure mode

Bad imitation becomes:
- bloodless corporate prose;
- fake sophistication;
- endless references to "markets" and "stakeholders";
- arch one-liners with no reporting underneath.

The cure is more mechanism, not more tone.
