## What the sources actually treat

Cohn investigates the representation of fictive consciousness through distinct linguistic modes. The selected section presents the narrated monologue, with Flaubert's *style indirect libre* central to its modern development. In free indirect discourse, third-person narrative grammar and the colouring of character consciousness can inhabit the **same sentence** without an explicit reporting clause such as 'she thought'. This is a grammatical/aesthetic phenomenon; it need not announce a theme or an arc.

The Cambridge review calls free indirect discourse sentences in third-person past narration that imitate a character's interior thinking, while stressing the experience of hearing a voice, not merely penetrating an internal psyche. It also notes that the name *free indirect discourse* is a technical scholarly label, not a full explanation of its felt experience.

Bakhtin, reading Dostoevsky, attends to discourse as relations among voices that should not automatically be subordinated to an authorial summary. This is a different entry: *how character speech and interpretation remain distinct*, not simply whether a narrator enters a mind. These accounts can potentially coexist; do not fabricate a disagreement.

## Immediate linguistic anchor

Free indirect discourse research directs attention to:
- **Grammatical person and tense**: third-person narration can present a character's proximal concern without quoting them directly.
- **Deictics and evaluative wording**: *now, here, surely, ridiculous* can reflect a fictional consciousness despite a narrating grammatical frame; actual attribution depends on context.
- **Reporting clause absence**: an unmarked shift can exist with no explicit 'she thought'. One cannot detect it by a single lexical trigger.
- **Attribution uncertainty**: a reader may not be able to conclusively say whether a judgment is narrator's, character's, or multiply voiced.

These are **questions and features described by the scholarly sources**, not a generated taxonomy of universal subtext techniques. Need exact original-language Flaubert passage and selected Cohn page to turn this into a fully grounded parallel-text study.

## Research discipline

Do not call ordinary lexical description 'free indirect discourse' merely because it describes a character. Do not infer an author's consciousness from a literary scholar's grammatical example. Important missing countercase: speech in which third-person past grammar is present but the evaluative stance is unmistakably external.
