# E07-03 — Strengthening words can silently strengthen claims

**2026-10-12, Expedition III.** **Status:** C2 comparative primary-text analysis for Strunk's 1920 examples, plus original candidate experimental materials. **No controlled test has been run.** Keep *grammar classification*, *readability*, *effect*, *semantic fidelity*, and *expressive intent* separate.

Primary source: William Strunk, *The Elements of Style*, chapter III §§10–11, 1920-text transcription https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/37134/pg37134-images.html . Related qualified criticism: [E07-02](E07-02-strunk-white-pullum-ferguson-at-the-grammatical-locus.md).

## Exact losses and gains worth tracking

| Strunk original -> revision | What the correction plausibly gains | What it can change or invent |
|---|---|---|
| **not much use** -> **useless** (Latin studies) | compact assertion, lexical decisiveness | total zero-value judgment replaces modest negative assessment |
| **many dead leaves lay on ground** -> **dead leaves covered ground** | subject acts immediately, clear scene | 'covered' suggests more continuous/extent-like distribution |
| guitar **could be heard** -> guitar **hummed sleepily** | sensory source and vividness | audibility/background experiencer removed; anthropomorphic subjective metaphor introduced |
| health **became impaired** -> failing health **compelled** college departure | explicit actor and immediate cause | causal force and necessity may be strengthened |
| **was very sorry** -> **repented** | short lexical predicate | remorse may acquire moral/penitential quality not established before |

These are **potential transformations, not proven reader effects**. The “many dead leaves” case may evoke roughly similar ground coverage in some contexts; judgement needs external readers.

## A small original test packet: context-sensitive counterexamples

These are **our deliberately constructed passages**, not disguised quotations from any historical source.

**Packet A — uncertainty preserving a weak claim:**

- Brief: A historian has weak evidence that a policy helped a small minority of farmers. Preserve scope and evidential uncertainty.
- Source: “The policy was not of much use to most farmers.”
- Candidate 1: “The policy was useless.”
- Candidate 2: “Most farmers gained little from the policy.”
- Candidate 3: “Farmers gained nothing from the policy.”

Comparison requires annotation of quantification (**most** vs all), degree (**little** vs none), and affected population. Candidate 2 might preserve the claim, although it still recasts what “use” means; 1/3 overstate it.

**Packet B — scene semantics / texture:**

- Brief: A character sees scattered leaves on a nearly clear pavement after a gust. The distribution must remain sparse.
- Source: “A great number of dead leaves lay along the pavement.”
- Candidate 1: “Dead leaves covered the pavement.”
- Candidate 2: “Dead leaves lay in clumps along the pavement.”
- Countercase: In an autumn forest carpeted in leaves, candidate 1 can be exactly apt; there is no global ban on **covered**.

**Packet C — grammar versus listener focus:**

- Brief 1: A police report is about *which victim* received attention. “The victim was interviewed by the officer” can preserve the victim as subject.
- Brief 2: A misconduct inquiry is about *what the officer did*. “The officer interviewed the victim” makes a different noun the entry point.
- Independent variables: identical event roles, different information structure and institutional stakes. The passive is not by itself evidence that agency is being concealed.

## Testing without overclaim

For each candidate collect independent outcomes:
- **Grammar:** actual voice/transitivity, not impressionistic “passive feel”.
- **Entailment / compatibility:** Does it commit to no value, some value, same event, same causal necessity?
- **Reader perception:** What is noticed first; what emotion is attributed; what ambiguity remains?
- **Fidelity:** What changed relative to the brief, including necessary vagueness?
- **Regime adequacy:** Is it a confidential report, novel, speech, press headline, satire?

Ask judges to supply concrete reasons and **counter-contexts**. A verdict of “more vigorous” alone is insufficient, but neither is a grammatical counterexample a full aesthetic verdict.

**Crucial experimental possibility:** a supposedly bad sentence can be exactly right once you know its communicative job. Conversely, an attractive edit can be misleading even when its grammar is impeccable. The value of observing that result is precisely that it could defeat STENDHAL's own assumed defaults.
