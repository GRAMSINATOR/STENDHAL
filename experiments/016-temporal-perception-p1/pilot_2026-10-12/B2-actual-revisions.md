## S — Pool / restrained short paragraph (139 words)

After the last swimmer had gone, Ivo checked the changing rooms, then made his way along the pool. A blue wristband floated at the shallow end. He would need the net from the plant room. He turned off the lights one by one, leaving only the strip over the water, and came back with the long handle under his arm.

The wristband was still where he'd seen it. Everything else on the surface had travelled towards the filters. He put the net beneath it, took it out, and waited while the water ran through the mesh. When he opened his hand the plastic had curled into the shape of someone's wrist. There was no mystery about that. Ivo laid it on the lost-property shelf, beside the towels people had forgotten to collect. The ventilation kept running in the dark.

## J — Bellwether / fictional first-person reported chronicle (438 words)

At eleven ten in the evening, the passenger across from me was trying to keep a cake box level on their knees. The bus leaned into a corner; they caught the sliding lid with two fingers. I watched for a moment, then looked up at the electronic display. Bellwether had added three evening routes for a six-week trial, running from eight until one in the morning. I was on one of them.

It would be easy to decide what the box meant. A birthday delivered after a shift. A late apology. A cake brought home for someone who had waited. None of those stories was available to me. What I could see was the box and the passenger's hand. What the city could count was a boarding.

The city reports that boardings rose by 24 percent compared with a matched baseline during the trial. In the same provisional figures, the share of recorded trips arriving on time fell from 81 percent to 72 percent. Those two movements belong together in the report, but they are not a contradiction. Demand and reliability answer different questions. A fuller evaluation would need to know whether the additional journeys were made possible by the later timetable, and where the lost punctuality occurred. The aggregate numbers don't say.

At a stop, the driver waited for someone approaching from the pavement. The passenger climbed aboard; the bus moved on. That is the entire observation. I cannot derive a general improvement in opportunity from one driver's patience, just as I cannot derive the social value of the trial from a single cake box.

The driver put it this way during the journey: “People don't stop working when the buses do.” It's a strong case for examining the hours a city chooses to serve. It is not a certificate of success for the route we were travelling on. The sentence tells me something about the reason to ask; the figures tell me part of what happened. Neither tells me who got home when they otherwise couldn't.

The next question is therefore narrower than whether the trial is good or bad. On which routes, at which hours, did boardings increase—and did those additional journeys arrive dependably enough to be useful? Bellwether has counted more people getting on and a smaller proportion of on-time arrivals. The relationship between those facts has not yet been described.

I never learned where the passenger with the cake box was going. I can describe the way they steadied the lid, but not the journey that made it necessary. That gap is where the next piece of reporting would have to begin.

## N — Clara / physically grounded expressive audition (529 words)

The attendant waited beside the door with his hands behind his back. He had told Clara to wait for a signal and now gave it with a small movement of his head, so small that she almost missed it. He looked relieved when she stepped through. Until that morning it had not occurred to her that someone else's job could consist entirely of telling people when they were allowed to begin.

The piano stood in the light, a pale upright instrument with a dull mark near the hinge of its lid. It had come from the loading area, where it had been left in the cold overnight. The attendant had explained this in a tone that made it clear no one was responsible for the weather. Beyond the lamps, three assessors were seated. Clara could see the corner of a desk, not a face.

She sat, straightened the music, placed her fingers on the keys. Cold. She lifted them at once. There was no reason to do that; cold keys were not broken keys. She put her hands down again, trying to recover the weight of the opening chord before playing it. She could feel each fingertip separately. It was the first thing she had felt distinctly since arriving.

Her teacher had always insisted on a moment's silence before the first note. The moment should belong to the player, he'd said, not to an examiner. Clara waited until the silence no longer felt like something she could own. Then she began.

Too fast. She knew it within six notes. Her hands were racing cold across the keys. The piano beating faster and faster beneath them. There was no point where she could seize the music and set it back at the correct speed without making the seizure audible; each phrase delivered the next one already moving. She kept her shoulders still. The sound had got ahead of her and she was following it, politely, like a person being shown around an unfamiliar building.

Someone beyond the lights coughed. It wasn't a verdict. She gave it one anyway. She wanted to turn towards the desk, to demonstrate that she understood how badly she was playing, but turning would have been a worse error and an even stranger kind of apology. She kept her eyes on the paper.

Near the bottom of the page were two bars of rest. She knew exactly where they were; she had never been afraid of them before. Now they approached while her hands went on working. They would require her to stop sending information across the room. She could not explain why that seemed more dangerous than playing too fast.

At the mark she lifted her fingers. The last chord took its time leaving the strings. No one said anything. A page shifted beyond the lights. Her hands, held above the keys, felt cold again. She counted the two bars and began where the score told her to begin. She had made it through the silence without discovering what the assessors thought. The attendant was no longer visible from the bench; nothing in the room was going to tell her when she could feel safe.
