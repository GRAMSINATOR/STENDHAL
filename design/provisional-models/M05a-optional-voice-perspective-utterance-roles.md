# M05a — Optional expression roles: textual voice ≠ perspective ≠ character ≠ dialogue

**Operator clarification, 2026-10-12.** **Status: research/design note for later investigation; explicitly NOT a built character model, comprehensive linguistic taxonomy, compulsory questionnaire, or runnable module.** Belongs beside [M05 adaptive decisions](M05-adaptive-craft-decision-topology.md), [M04 scoped tonal field](M04-scoped-tonal-field.md), [M03 transformative process](M03-transformative-composition-process.md). Preserve as a separable provisional branch.

## The originating requirement

STENDHAL is a writing model **capable of any kind of composition**, not an implicit novel-writing program.

The operator contrasts:
- an ordinary press article with **no character perspective**, although it has an article-level expressive or editorial voice;
- a first-person journalistic chronicle where a specific journalist's situated account enters the work;
- an article that shifts temporarily into remembered/scene-like narration and then returns to first-person commentary or analysis;
- a narrative with no characters, one perspective, many perspectives, or multiple character embodiments;
- a story with one continuing **focal perspective** yet numerous people speaking with markedly different **dialogue voices**;
- expression whose communicative aims, emotional acts and social stakes are **not identical** to its underlying perception/perspective.

**The operator explicitly requested that character dialogue embodiment be investigated later, not prematurely built in this turn.**

## Independence of the candidate distinctions

These are different *questions* about a writing event, not claims that all distinct entities must be represented as separate rigid objects.

### 1. Discourse regime / medium

Article, first-person report, argumentative essay, scientific explanation, lyrical essay, scripted conversation, fiction, characterless narrative, etc. This may establish factual obligations and default reader expectations; it **does not determine** whether a character, persona or first-person viewpoint must exist.

### 2. Textual voice / register / stance

How the language comes across: editorial sobriety, compressed lyric, polemical essay voice, humorous chronicle, neutral-seeming information delivery, authorial intrusion, etc. A text with **no dramatised character** still has some linguistic texture, whether marked or unmarked. This is not proof of an individuated speaker personality. Voice can be stable across a whole article or locally shift.

### 3. Perspective / focalisation / situated epistemics

What perceiver's knowledge, sensory access, evaluation, priorities or interpretive orientation informs a span. “Perspective is subtext of expression,” in the operator's phrasing: it can shape expression without appearing as quotation or a recognisable speech manner. It may be embodied, inferred, explicitly claimed, impersonal or indeterminate. **No forced one-to-one alignment of first-person grammar, focal access and voice.**

### 4. Character / persona / embodiment

Does the work construct a specifically situated individual with some continuity of world-model, psychology, social position, history or bodily presence? A real journalist reporting as “I” is not automatically a fictional character; a quoted scientist is a real source, not necessarily a dramatised literary personality. A fictional first-person narrator is not identical to the empirical author.

**Character count can be zero.** Narrating a scene does not prove there is a character perspective. Conversely a single focal perspective does not imply only one speaking voice.

### 5. Utterance voice (including dialogue) and interactive action

When someone speaks or an utterance is represented, ask separately:
- Who is represented as uttering these words?
- To whom, if anyone, are they addressed, and under what social relation?
- What is literally said, implied, withheld, or emotionally enacted?
- What **communicative action** is attempted or accomplished (informing, appeasing, refusing, insulting, performing authority, confessing, destabilising, joking, seeking recognition)?
- Does the speaker's register, diction, turn-taking, rhythm or idiom diverge from the narrator and from their own usual speech?
- Is the utterance direct speech, indirect report, mediated quotation, free indirect representation, dialogue, monologue, thought or text with ambiguous source?

Speech **is not the same thing** as focal perspective. A character whose mind structures a scene can hear many other distinct speakers. Utterances can run counter to speakers' private views or have several incompatible purposes. Expression may be utilitarian, expressive, relational, ritual, deceptive, or several at once; don't reduce every line to a single utilitarian goal.

### 6. Frame and mode transitions

A first-person journalist might move:

```text
present-tense analysis / column voice
  → recollected event / scene
  → reported quotation / witness voice
  → the writer's retrospective interpretation
  → present argument / analysis again
```

The switch may involve tense and focal access but need not create new dramatic characters. It can last a phrase, paragraph, section or whole work. Frames and voices can overlap, especially in free indirect discourse.

## Conditional entry questions for the decision graph

**Do NOT install “How many characters?” as an unconditional top-level decision.** This would bake novel-writing assumptions into reportage, analysis, poetry and other modes.

Better candidates, called **only if relevant**, with guards still to be researched:

| Candidate question | When useful | When to skip or defer |
|---|---|---|
| What communicative regime / reader contract applies? | Writing/revising in any substantive context | Can stay implicit for exploratory fragments |
| What expressive sources are relevant here, if any? | Distinct narrator, source, witness, persona, quoted participant | Ordinary technical report may not need individuated voices |
| Is focal access / situated interpretation active? | Perception-laden prose, chronicle, essay position, narration | Impersonal instruction or pure formal definition may not need person-bound focalisation |
| Is any sustained character or persona *constructed*? | Stories, profiles, dramatized memoir, some essays | News briefs, non-dramatic criticism, characterless narratives |
| Is an utterance or interaction represented? | Dialogue, interview, quotations, monologue, inner speech | Non-utterance spans; don't force a dialogue pass |
| What does an utterance accomplish, beyond what it says? | Speech interaction and communicative stakes | Not every represented text needs inferred deception/persuasion |
| Are there changes of discursive frame or speaking/focal source? | Embedded narrative, quotation, analysis→scene, polyphonic fiction | Stable voice; unnecessary if no shift |
| What persists across frame changes and what legitimately changes? | Maintaining coherence, contrast, attribution, stance | If no such framing obligation exists |
| Do local voice/utterance commitments alter the next creative intention? | Within M03 perception→reaction→reframe loop | No mandatory output evaluation for every sentence |

These are **opportunity questions, not a compulsory decision tree**. A useful harness might use a simple role/frame locator before deciding which of these deeper tools are worth activating, but that locator itself needs evidence from the actual text.

## Illustrative orthogonal cases (analyst-authored, NOT corpus results)

**A — factual explanatory article:** impersonal editorial voice, multiple attributed facts, no fabricated characters or dramatised narrator. Requiring dialogue characterization here is a category mistake.

**B — first-person journalistic chronicle:** a stable journalist's commentary is interrupted by a recalled scene, another person's direct quotation, and a return to analysis. One identity can appear across multiple *frames*, while the quoted utterance carries a distinct source and social purpose. All reported facts and quotations remain subject to applicable evidential obligations.

**C — single-focalizer narrative with many voices:** a scene is focalized largely through one protagonist, while a friend pleads, a clerk offers bureaucratic reassurance, and a stranger jokes. Distinct **dialogue manners / speech acts** need not entail shifting focal perspective among the speakers.

**D — characterless narrative:** a changing physical landscape, an inanimate environment, a collective historical process or a conceptual/lyrical unfolding can have vivid voice, timing and perspective without identifiable character psychology.

**E — direct quotation in press:** real interviewee's word choice can introduce a local voice, but the author shouldn't invent a fictional “character arc” or silently change the quoted speaker's claims.

## Genuine dangers

- Confusing grammatical first person with an intentional fictional persona or an autobiographically trustworthy speaker.
- Treating every voice shift as a point-of-view shift or every POV shift as a distinct dialogue style.
- Equating social dialogue with functionless ornament, or collapsing it into purely transactional utility.
- Treating the journalist's quoted subject as a fiction character and embellishing their speech.
- Forcing one perspective per paragraph, though nested quotation and free indirect narration can blend sources.
- Rigidly assigning a book or article one voice when its frames change at differing scales.
- Creating innumerable “character metadata” fields when the task has no characters.
- Treating speaker intention and achieved reader effect as identical; [intention/outcome reciprocity](../../vault/parallel-streams/2026-10-12-02-intention-outcome-autopoiesis-and-partial-control.md) still applies.
