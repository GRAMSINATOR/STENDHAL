# PR03 — Hamlet: Coleridge, Eliot, C. S. Lewis disagree over the object of criticism

**Status: three-critic primary-source triangulation, 2026-10-12.** Three distinguished literary minds, with Shakespeare's play as common object. This is a study of attested disagreements **about perception and artistic composition**, not a verdict.

## Coleridge — what he actually says

In the lecture, Coleridge describes Hamlet as a character whose extraordinarily active inner thought and imagination unsettle the equilibrium between perceptions of external things and reflection on one's own mental life. This gives Hamlet's contemplation and image-making unusual dominance over action; Coleridge explicitly contrasts the slowness of *Hamlet* with the concentrated movement of *Macbeth*.

Coleridge approaches the play as a psychologically interpretable **representation of a particular human being**. He insists that apparent inconsistency need not imply Shakespearean ineptitude; the character can be traced to serious knowledge of mind.

**Grounded facet:** a reading organised around mental causation and the discrepancy between inward imaginative activity and outward action. This is **not** a verified account of how actual historical people process decisions.

## Eliot — what he actually says about Coleridge and Hamlet

Eliot opens by calling criticism that substitutes the interpreter's self for the play dangerous. He specifically accuses Goethe and Coleridge of producing portraits of their own sensibilities while purporting to understand Hamlet.

Eliot shifts from *the prince* to *the play*, hypothesises an imperfect rewriting of older revenge materials, notes what he sees as superfluous Polonius scenes and inconsistencies, and judges the drama artistically unsuccessful. The relation between an emotion and the staged objects/actions evoking it is his famous "objective correlative" criterion. Hamlet's emotion is, according to Eliot, inadequately objectified in the dramatic conditions.

**Grounded facet:** criticism of a work's overall arrangement and adequacy to an emotion, not only psychological coherence of its protagonist.

**Crucial historical qualifier:** Eliot's reconstruction of earlier *Hamlet* material relies on Robertson, a lost play and conjectures; his claims about Shakespeare's private emotional difficulty are themselves speculative. His attack on Coleridge is partly a charge about **the proper unit of criticism**, not a mere disagreement about whether Hamlet is interesting.

## Lewis — directly responds to Eliot and the earlier psychology tradition

Lewis organises his 1942 lecture around sharply conflicting schools of criticism:
1. Critics who think Hamlet's conduct is unmotivated and the play defective; he names Eliot among them.
2. Critics who think Hamlet acts as quickly as circumstances permit.
3. Critics who think Hamlet delays for psychological reasons, a broad family including Coleridge and Goethe.

Lewis observes that this leaves critics of recognised sensitivity agreeing on the central importance of the work while disagreeing profoundly about its supposed central character. He explicitly treats Eliot's 'artistic failure' conclusion as a serious option and explores why it remains unsatisfactory to him.

Lewis then reframes the site of interest: what stays with him is not a portrait of Hamlet's specific personality so much as an entire imaginative experience of uncertainty, death, spectral visitation, disorientation and a world difficult to know. He compares Shakespeare's speech to operatic song: **the representational medium is not necessarily a literal description of the fictive speaker's own artistic talent**. At one point Lewis says Eliot's own poetry is closer to this underlying method than Eliot suspects.

**A real and partially sympathetic reply:** Lewis agrees that the text does not give enough causal information to sustain a tidy psychological explanation for all Hamlet's delays, but he disagrees that this lack is necessarily the defect Eliot takes it to be.

**Lewis's explicit limitation:** he admits the play might indeed have radical defects; his objection is to the certainty with which some critics announce artistic failure. His positive reading is his own critical vision, not a controlled empirical demonstration.

## Comparison — disagreement at several different places

| Critical location | Coleridge | Eliot | Lewis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Most relevant object | Hamlet's psychology, internal-external imbalance | Artistic unity and adequate dramatic expression | The poem/play's imaginative experience in the reader |
| Apparent inconsistency/delay | An intelligible result of reflective overactivity | A sign of intractable and imperfectly combined material | May be underexplained without thereby invalidating the work |
| What deserves close attention | Thoughts, images, hesitation and mental constitution | Source layers, dramatic situation, verbal and structural integration | Recurrent images, atmosphere, speech as medium, sustained uncertainty |
| What the other critics may miss | Psychological coherence | The play as artwork rather than projection of self | The felt world of the play rather than a reconstructed single-person psychology |
| Strength of historical claim | Account of depicted psychology, attributed to Shakespeare's insight | Conjectural history of earlier play and creator's emotion | Interpretation of reading experience, not Shakespeare's secret motives |

**No need to declare a winner.** Observe that Lewis criticises both a simple psychological decoding and Eliot's categorical negative judgment, while partly agreeing with Eliot on underdetermination.

## Passage-level anchors for later deeper work

1. **Hamlet 1.2 (first soliloquy):** the body, dissolution and an image of the world as uncontrolled growth. Lewis cites these as contributions to a larger experiential region; Coleridge makes inward imaginative preoccupation central.
2. **Hamlet 2.2 (Rosencrantz/Guildenstern):** the difference between perceiving a world and depicting a particular speaker's character is central to Lewis's example about the vastness of the heavens and man.
3. **Hamlet 3.1 (soliloquy):** death's unknown condition matters to Lewis as a pervasive experiential problem rather than one psychological trait.
4. **Hamlet 2.1 (Polonius–Reynaldo):** Eliot questions the scene's contribution to the whole. A separate [paired reading](PR02-hamlet-eliot-polonius.md) identifies Polonius's indirect surveillance method, but **neither Coleridge nor Lewis here directly refutes Eliot's Polonius-specific objection**. The broad disagreement does not become fabricated micro-level consensus.

## Meta-critical observation grounded in this exchange

**The disputed dimensions are not always competing observations at the same level.** Three accomplished readers may look at psychology, form and reader experience, respectively, each with vivid original insight, while disputing which is artistically decisive. That is precisely why simple technique checklists and a single critical lens would miss real material.

This is a conclusion about the *documented divergence of their arguments*, not proof that one perceptual decomposition is universally superior.
