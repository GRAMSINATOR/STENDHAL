## What Eliot specifically argues

Eliot calls the *play* an artistic failure (while acknowledging its deep interest). He hypothesises Shakespeare overlaid an inherited revenge play with a second emotional motive, especially Hamlet's disgust about his mother, without finding dramatic material adequate to express it. Eliot relies on J. M. Robertson's historical conjectures and on the idea of an 'objective correlative'—a set of external dramatic circumstances capable of evoking the specific artistic emotion. He names the Polonius–Laertes and Polonius–Reynaldo scenes as unexplained and of dubious structural/style provenance.

**Separate statuses:** Eliot's judgment is direct; his attribution of earlier scenes to Kyd, Chapman or lost earlier versions is historically conjectural, mediated by Robertson, and not established by the passage alone.

## The criticised scene in the play

In 2.1, Polonius instructs Reynaldo to question acquaintances in Paris about Laertes. His proposed method involves deliberately introducing plausible minor slanders and observing the replies to infer the truth. His strategy is itself summarised in terms of finding directions through indirections (Folger around lines 975–1005). He repeatedly interrupts his own explanation with clarifications and subsidiary examples.

Immediately afterward, Ophelia enters distressed by Hamlet's conduct. Polonius leaps toward a confident explanation in terms of rejected love and decides to bring it to the king. Later, in Act 3 Scene 1, he participates in staging an encounter for the purpose of secretly observing Hamlet.

## What can be observed without deciding whether the scene is necessary

- It explicitly depicts Polonius **instructing a subordinate in indirect surveillance** and attempting to calibrate misinformation for intelligence gathering.
- It displays his discursive manner at length, including wandering precision, self-correction and insistence on being understood.
- Its sequence sits next to Ophelia's report and Polonius's interpretation of Hamlet's state. This juxtaposition is **textual fact**; whether it establishes deliberate irony or formal necessity is interpretive.
- The later spying episode establishes a **repeated action pattern** at larger scale, whether or not every element of the Reynaldo scene is required for the plot.
- Calling it 'superfluous' is a statement about its marginal contribution to some chosen model of the whole play. It is not a direct description of what the scene contains.

## A real unresolved critical question

Eliot does not say the scene contains nothing; his historical/structural claim is that its presence in the inherited material does not integrate adequately into the artistic whole **as he construes that whole**. A defender might find its depiction of Polonius's method/voice revealing and its adjacency to Ophelia's report consequential, but that is not yet a sourced expert counterargument.
