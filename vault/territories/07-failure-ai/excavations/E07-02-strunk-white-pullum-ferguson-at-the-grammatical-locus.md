## What Strunk actually said (and did not say)

The original §10 title is “Use the active voice.” But *inside* it the writer explicitly states that the rule **does not** mean discarding passives entirely, acknowledging that they can be “frequently convenient and sometimes necessary.” He immediately offers a genuinely informative discourse-context distinction:

- Restored dramaturgical context favours having Restoration dramatists occupy the sentence subject (passive), while a discussion of modern readers favours an active clause about readers.

Therefore an investigator who reports Strunk as holding “every passive is wrong” has misdescribed his **actual articulated claim**.

### The original comparison doing a lot of suspicious work

Strunk's active “I shall always remember my first visit to Boston” versus a passive in which the same content “will always be remembered by me.” Pullum points out that *by me* is pragmatically infelicitous in ordinary unconstrained first-person memory talk: the agent is already known by the fact of the utterance. This is an argument about **information structure / discourse-newness**, not a denial that some passives are clumsy.

Pullum gives a contrastive context where *by me* naturally works (“not painted by Picasso ... painted by me”). This directly limits the validity of Strunk's example as a **representative** case for all passives.

## The four pairs: what the heading hides

In Strunk's 1920 witness, the prose moves from the passive discussion to replacing 'perfunctory' presentational expressions with active verbs. The examples include:

| Original structure (brief anchor) | New structure (brief anchor) | Complication |
|---|---|---|
| existential **There were ... dead leaves** | leaves **covered** the ground | No passive to remove from the original; revision also increases the suggestion of coverage/density |
| a guitar sound **could be heard** | guitar **hummed sleepily** | Passive replaced, but sound is now given a subjective metaphor; hearing/experience is differently represented |
| health **became impaired** | failing health **compelled** departure | “became impaired” is not a passive clause; new clause asserts stronger causal compulsion |
| **It was not long** before regret | **soon repented** words | Initial clause isn't passive; 'repented' may suggest moral or penitential force beyond feeling sorry |

Thus Pullum's grammatical claim—that three originals are not passives—is right. **That does not prove** they were explicitly intended by Strunk/White *as four examples of passive voice*. Ferguson's 2009 defence focuses exactly on this ambiguity, arguing that they illustrate replacing stylistically weak constructions with stronger verbs rather than a passive-only conversion.

### The actual scholarly friction

- **Pullum:** Given the “Use the active voice” heading and the sentence about substituting active transitive verbs, the worked examples confuse categories; even if a broader claim was intended, the instruction's syntax and rhetoric remain misleading. He also observes that a later White-introduced “cock's crow came with dawn” has an **intransitive** verb, contrary to an exact instruction invoking transitivity.
- **Ferguson:** The target is not every grammatical passive but stylistic limpness; Pullum has interpreted the illustrative set too narrowly; the manual already qualifies its advice.

Both arguments should be recorded as *claims about the same passage*, not as a forced binary of modern linguist right versus literary practitioner wrong.

### Why the actual edition matters

The **1920** Strunk transcription says “substituting a **verb** in the active voice”; Pullum cites the **2000 fourth edition** saying “substituting a **transitive** in the active voice”. These are **not the same exact sentence**, and White's later examples also differ (guitar/falls/rooster/cock across witnesses). Another accessible scan purporting to preserve earlier wording says “transitive”; authentic edition-by-edition facsimile collation is required before attributing the shift to a named reviser. If a critic and defender silently read different editions, that is a source-critical variable, not evidence about a unique unchanging manual.

## A second case inside Strunk that is much harder to defend by grammatical labels

§11 “Put statements in positive form” pairs a sentence saying that studying Latin is **not much use** with “the study of Latin [is] useless”. This change is rhetorically sharper but logically stronger: *low utility* need not mean *zero utility*. Under appropriate context, asserting the latter can be false while the former is true.

A later example compresses a qualified judgment of *The Taming of the Shrew* into a categorical description of its women. Strunk explicitly admits: the “corrected version ... is simply a guess at the writer's intention.” **This self-awareness matters:** the manual recognises that stronger syntax and recovered intent are not the same achievement.

This directly connects to [E05-02, Gopen & Swan](../../05-science-epistemics/excavations/E05-02-gopen-swan-two-revisions-epistemic-risk.md), where clearer scientific revisions make differing interpretations of research priorities. The textually supported similarity is a **fidelity hazard**, not shared cognitive machinery.

## The genuinely useful failure modes

1. **Category error:** a particular sentence is called passive even though its grammar isn't passive.
2. **Context omission:** a structural example is evaluated as a context-free universal, where the source itself recognises situational variation.
3. **Critical scope creep:** an admittedly imprecise demonstration is taken as proof that its authors lack any useful stylistic perception.
4. **Semantic escalation:** strengthening a sentence converts weak assertion to strong assertion without authorial warrant.
5. **Witness contamination:** an edition's precise wording is inadvertently attributed to another edition.
6. **Taste-to-fact leap:** a critic's assessment that a sentence is “vigorous” is treated as a measurable grammatical fact, rather than a reader/regime-dependent judgment.

**These are source-specific observations and candidate risks, not six categories of all failed criticism.**

## A close-reading protocol, without a new style law

For each quotation or correction record:
- Exact source edition and *what the passage actually asserts*.
- Grammatical description independently checked.
- What the revision changes besides the named target.
- A counter-context where the alleged weakness would be functional or desirable.
- Whether the critic's diagnosis survives the strongest good-faith rival reading.
- What remains dependent on intention or reader response.

Do not decide that 'The Elements of Style' is all right or all wrong. Do not import a global anti-prescriptivism reflex either.
