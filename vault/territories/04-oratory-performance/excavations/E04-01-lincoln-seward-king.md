# Region 04 — oral prose and actual wording changes: Lincoln, Seward and King

**Status:** primary-source archival page reading, plus university archival history. Stronger than merely indexing a speechwriter's advice.

## Actual primary evidence: Seward, then Lincoln, 1861

**Library of Congress, Lincoln's First Inaugural Address:**
- Seward's handwritten concluding proposal and transcription: https://loc.gov/exhibits/lincoln/ext/transcription13.html
- Lincoln's final concluding text: https://loc.gov/exhibits/lincoln/ext/transcription14.html
- Full scholarly archival collation of draft states, authors and amendments: https://www.loc.gov/resource/mal.0773800/?st=text
- Overview of the First Inaugural revisions: https://wwws.loc.gov/exhibits/lincoln/the-sixteenth-president.html

**Source status:** C2 for LOC original archival transcriptions and collation. The LOC's attributions of particular revisions to Seward/Lincoln are documented editorial findings, not based on our own palaeography.

The earlier suggestion and final wording share *mystic chords* and images of battlefields, graves, memory and hearts/hearths. Lincoln's final formulation shifts the ending from Seward's singular guardian angel of the nation to **'better angels of our nature'** and transforms the syntax around musical swelling/chorus. The final version also directly says *We are not enemies, but friends*.

**Material-level observations:** plural/singular substitution; final collective pronoun *our*; metaphor retained but recontextualised; musical vocabulary and movement retained but reordered; short declarative before extended rhythmic accumulation. These are literally visible in the witnesses. The emotional consequences and exact conscious deliberation are **not** proved from transcript alone.

The LOC says Seward suggested many alterations that Lincoln adopted selectively; edits were made late, including cut-and-pasted pieces for ease of delivery. This is documentary evidence of **collaborative verbal composition**.

## King — written speech versus live performance

**Stanford King's Institute**: https://kinginstitute.stanford.edu/i-have-dream

The historical commentary details how King drew on his own prior sermon/speech expressions over years and received collaborators' suggestions. It identifies Clarence Jones's contribution to a promissory-note metaphor and differentiates the prepared typescript from what King spoke in August 1963. **C2 direct institute archival history; does not replace complete typescript/audio collation.**

This is an important constraint on a purely page-based study: performance can create or alter actual wording, audience response, timing and prosody. A printed transcript may obscure the moment in which the speaker departs from the written script.

## Practitioner voice: Ted Sorensen

A widely reproduced extract from Sorensen's discussion of JFK speechwriting emphasises short clauses, audible comprehension, alliteration, rhythm and unconventional leading conjunctions/dashes: https://www.thoughtco.com/ted-sorensen-on-speech-writing-1691843 . **C1 secondary reproduction of Sorensen; original edition/pagination not yet confirmed**. Treat as an acquisition lead, not direct verified quote.

## Critical distinctions

Speech text, speech heard, rhetorical effect, collaborator's suggestion, and later political reception are **not the same artefact**. The strongest current case is the Seward–Lincoln side-by-side archived wording, not any invented theory that all successful speech has three beats.
