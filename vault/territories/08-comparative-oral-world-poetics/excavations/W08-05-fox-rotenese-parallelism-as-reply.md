# W08-05 — The paired line can reply: Rotenese canonical parallelism in active conversation

**Parallel-stream excavation, 2026-10-12.** **Evidence status:** C2 — read James J. Fox's own scholarly fieldwork narrative and small parallel-language transcription in an ANU Press HTML edition; C0 for direct acoustic/performance analysis (recordings not heard) and independent original-language grammatical analysis.

## Source and circumstances

James J. Fox, *Explorations in Semantic Parallelism*, chapter 10, “Manu Kama's Road, Tepa Nilu's Path,” freely available chapter: https://press-files.anu.edu.au/downloads/press/p284951/html/Text/ch10.xhtml .

Fox investigates ritual compositions from Rote, eastern Indonesia. His source history is especially important: Jonker's 1911 publication contained an earlier collected text (*Manu Kama ma Tepa Nilu*); Fox brought it to Rote in 1965 and later questioned the meaning of the recurring orphan/widow categories. A chanter, Meno, subsequently inserted an answering passage into a performance of a **different** chant, *Lilo Tola ma Koli Lusi*. This is a scholarly witness's account of a performer responding through inherited formulaic language; it is **not** identical with possession of the unmediated performance event.

## Minimal primary-language locus

The small recorded sequence cycles between two categories. Two lines from the reported intervention are:

- **Se ana-mak?**
- **Ma se falu-ina?**

Fox's English translation frames these as parallel questions concerning who counts as an orphan and who counts as a widow; intervening and subsequent responses extend the identification to **everyone**. The second category is not just a replacement synonym. Together they develop a culturally meaningful paired description of human vulnerability and mortality.

The language-level pair, repeated universal responses, and parallel question frame matter. We should not confidently assign every Rote morpheme a function from English alone. The original passage continues into a pair of named roads/paths and elaborates on shared human fate.

## What changes our picture of writing

1. **Parallelism is neither redundancy nor merely ornament:** correspondence sets up an expectation, but the second line can alter the conceptual extension of the first. A paraphrase that collapses the pair into “all humans are mortal” removes the compositional event.
2. **An inherited pattern can carry new situational meaning:** Meno's reported interpolation addresses an ethnographer's earlier question by redeploying recognised ritual language. Formal recurrence does not entail intellectual stasis.
3. **Meaning extends outside the isolated utterance:** the earlier 1911 text, Fox's subsequent question, later performance, audience knowledge and the new verbal response form an episode. A page-only comparison misses the time-dependent act of reply.
4. **There is constraint and improvisation at once:** conventional dyadic lexical relations supply strong compositional resources while a performer can make a locally responsive choice. It would be wrong to equate fixed pairs with a single fixed poem.
5. **Translation preserves some parallel syntax but not all sound and social indexing:** English “and” and matched nouns permit a structural reading, not reconstruction of the original recital's pitch, tempo, phonetic detail or audience uptake.

## Expert methodological disagreement to preserve

The existing W08-03 documents Parry–Lord's metrical/formulaic emphasis and Fox's argument for traditions whose working constraints are **canonical paired lexemes**. Fox need not deny observed South Slavic metrical formulas. The technical contention is **which kind of recurrent unit** explains distinct compositional traditions.

Do not promote “repetition is good” or “use parallel couples” as universal writing advice. A study of deliberate **anti-parallelism** is still needed.
