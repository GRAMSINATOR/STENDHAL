# E03-02 — Austen's carriage passage: grammatical continuity, migrating felt voice

**Parallel stream 02; 2026-10-12.** **Evidence:** C2 for the primary *Emma* passage; C1 for specific modern scholarly abstracts on interpretation; C0 for manuscripts/copy-text provenance of online transcription variants.

## Exact source locus and contexts

Jane Austen, *Emma* (1815), volume III chapter VII (modern continuous numbering chapter 43), moments after Mr Knightley publicly challenges Emma's cruel remark to Miss Bates at Box Hill. Two openly available witnesses:
- Wikisource volume III chapter VII: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Emma_(Austen)/Volume_3/Chapter_7
- Project Gutenberg text (updated 2026), same passage: https://www.gutenberg.org/files/158/158-h/158-h.htm

The paragraphs move from Knightley's misconstrual of Emma's silence to Emma leaning from the carriage, losing sight of him, reporting shame, then undergoing a sequence of accusatory, exclamatory questions. Small original anchors:
- `with what appeared unusual speed` — an external event filtered through her felt time;
- `How could she have been so brutal` — unquoted third-person self-reproach.

No part of the narrative suddenly announces “Emma thought:”. Yet the reader increasingly hears **her** thinking in the narratorial surface.

## Material sequence, not a mechanical “POV switch”

1. **External action:** moving carriage, backward gaze, distance, horses. The physical scene grounds the psychological movement.
2. **Perceptual inflection:** “appeared unusual speed” transfers a judgment of time/distance into the narrative without stating that time really accelerated.
3. **Narrated agitation:** her mortification is reported in the past tense, initially with obvious narratorial mediation.
4. **Free indirect exclamations:** three cumulative reproaches retain third-person forms but enact her live evaluative urgency; repetition of “How could…” has rhetorical energy beyond reporting “she regretted it.”
5. **Return to reported state:** “Time did not compose her” and the quiet journey close the passage into a narrator-led descriptive frame.

The characteristic operation is a **gradient in voice-source and proximity**, not the simple presence of a third-person pronoun. The narrated frame and Emma's subjectivity can jointly inhabit the sentence; exact attribution of a subphrase can be ambiguous.

## Small but important witness discrepancy

At the same point, Wikisource gives `The truth of **his** representation there was no denying`, while Gutenberg prints `The truth of **this** representation there was no denying`. Wikisource also has an em dash after the first accusatory question while the Gutenberg rendering does not. **These are online-witness differences only.** We have *not* established which reflects 1815/1816 first printing, an editorial emendation, or a transcription choice. A close reader must not declare an authorial stylistic cause for a difference in digital witnesses.

## Scholarly lenses with source limits

- Daniel P. Gunn, “Free Indirect Discourse,” *Oxford Research Encyclopedia of Literature* (2019), abstract: https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61883/chapter-abstract/547772880 . Its definition includes a character's judgments and deictics entering the narrator's discourse without a new attribution frame or wholesale person/tense shift. **C1 abstract, not full paper.**
- Rachel Provenzano Oberman, “Fused Voices: Narrated Monologue in Jane Austen's Emma,” *Nineteenth-Century Literature* 64(1), 2009, pp. 1–15, abstract: https://online.ucpress.edu/ncl/article-abstract/64/1/1/67583/Fused-Voices-Narrated-Monologue-in-Jane-Austen-s . She argues Emma's developing ability to incorporate other perspectives has a structural analogy to narration's mixed-voice capacity. **C1 abstract, not passage-level demonstration verified.**
- “Misreading Emma,” chapter abstract, Oxford Scholarship Online: https://academic.oup.com/book/3967/chapter-abstract/145590238 . This argues against the assumption that FID automatically hides Emma's mistaken inferences and suggests readers can receive clues to her error. **C1 abstract only, not an evidenced expert dispute on this particular carriage paragraph.**
- University of Nebraska–Lincoln's Austen speech database codes the sequence as “Narrator as Emma Woodhouse” in its searchable records: https://austen.unl.edu/search?character_type=heroine&class_status=&novel=emma&page=6&rows=50 . This is independent **editorial classification**, not a fact of neurological reader perception.

## Preservation/loss experiment (original counterfactuals)

Compare three *short independently written reconstructions of the situation*:
- Narrator-dominant: “In the carriage Emma regretted having humiliated Miss Bates and offending Knightley.”
- Character-dominant, directly quoted: “How could I have done that? How could I face Miss Bates?”
- Free-indirect attempt: “How could she have done it? Of all people, Miss Bates—and he had seen it.”

These test versions are **not Austen's text**; none reproduces her cadence, period idiom, proportion or full context. Yet they help isolate differences in narrator responsibility, immediate pressure, pronoun/tense and reader inference.

A bare statement of Emma's regret preserves an event-summary but strips the experience of arriving at self-condemnation. A first-person monologue preserves direct feeling but changes who is formally presented as speaking. An indiscriminate “make FID stronger” algorithm may lose the narrator's ironic distance.

## Questions for serious further reading

Does every reader attribute every exclamation solely to Emma? How do punctuation differences change perceptual boundaries? Are direct quotation and FID reliably different in silent reading, audiobook performance, or translation? What happens when a narrator uses FID to make the character *less* trustworthy?

**Do not make “voice permeability” an untested axis or mandatory meter.** The local close reading is valuable; a continuous representation would be a later design hypothesis.
