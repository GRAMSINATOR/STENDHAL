## An actually documented example — the river

The Stanford entry examines the example: **“The village is on the Ganges.”** The literal physical location seems impossible if read strictly; secondary meaning can place it on the bank. On Ānandavardhana's account, further *suggestion* may evoke an association of **purity** (and, in later commentary, coolness) tied to the sacred river, beyond the necessary referential repair.

The example is valuable because it distinguishes:
- ordinary designated meaning;
- secondary semantic adjustment to get an intelligible statement;
- culturally structured suggested significance that is **not simply the adjustment itself**.

**Qualification:** The sentence and suggested quality are known here from philosophical exegesis; they are not evidence of every contemporary reader actually entertaining purity. Culture and source context matter.

## A surprising scale claim in the tradition

The Stanford entry explicitly reports that the theory distinguishes suggestion arising at several levels: **phonemes**, words, sentences, discourse units, and even relations sustained across a poem. It also notes that Ānandavardhana permits indefinite combinations instead of supposing a complete enumeration of kinds of literary suggestion.

This is relevant to our multiscale perceptual ambition *as historical evidence of a sophisticated independent theoretical tradition*. It is **not** proof that Sanskrit theory maps directly onto English genre classification or that we have already seen the actual original Sanskrit phoneme effects.

## Distinguished internal opponents and developments

The SEP describes an objection from thinkers who believe proposed suggestion can be explained by existing semantic or inferential resources. Some proponents dispute exactly what is being added beyond those resources. Abhinavagupta's later interpretation places greater emphasis on the **reader's emotional experience** in relation to rasa, unlike a simpler reading in which rasa is an aesthetic content in the work.
