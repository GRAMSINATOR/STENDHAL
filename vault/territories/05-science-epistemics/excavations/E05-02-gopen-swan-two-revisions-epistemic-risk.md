# E05-02 — One syntactic problem, two incompatible revisionary commitments

**Parallel-stream excavation, 2026-10-12.** **Evidence status:** C2 — targeted close reading of Gopen & Swan's original 1990 article in university-hosted HTML transcription; C0 for independent biomedical verification. The study is a craft demonstration, **not** a controlled experimental evaluation of universal reader behaviour.

## Primary source and passage

George D. Gopen and Judith A. Swan, “The Science of Scientific Writing,” *American Scientist* 78(6), 1990, pp. 550–558, accessible faithful text reproduction at Penn State: https://courses.ems.psu.edu/styleforstudents/print/c10_p6.html .

Target locus: the opening sentence of the authors' example paragraph about human mitochondrial URFs and their correspondence to a yeast ATPase gene. The text states that the first sentence is 42 words and that **23 words separate the grammatical subject from its main verb**. This count is the **authors' claim** (some third-party teaching summaries give different interruption counts).

Structural skeleton, *our short abstract*, not an invented original quotation:

> [The smallest URF] — [long classification and relational identification] — [has been identified as a yeast-gene equivalent].

The potentially important molecular description sits inside a noun-phrase interruption before the reader discovers the sentence's predicate.

## The authors deliberately generate two different repairs

**Route A, retain description as an informational act.** Promote the descriptive insertion to the predicate of one independent segment and use a semicolon to establish another segment with its own closure. In effect: identify *which URF this is* as its own proposition, then present its reported yeast-gene equivalence. The long scientific detail is preserved and granted structural prominence.

**Route B, subordinate or delete the description.** Reduce the identification to a compact apposition and send the sentence directly to the homology/equivalence claim. Now the sentence's dominant job is different. This is not just a shorter version of the same stress architecture.

The crucial sentence in the original argument acknowledges that **the original author would have to decide which alternative better expresses the intended priority**. Later Gopen and Swan explicitly admit that a clearer revision reflects their *interpretation* rather than necessarily the source researcher's intention. They also state that their principles are not fixed writing rules and can be violated deliberately.

## Microscopic observations

- **Subject–predicate distance:** is only one measurable local property; shortening it does not specify the correct scientific priority.
- **Semantic foregrounding:** Route A names the molecular identification as foregrounded content, where Route B permits it to recede. Two syntactically clearer sentences enact different assessments of what matters.
- **Two stress opportunities:** Route A's semicolon creates an intermediate syntactic closure and a terminal one. Punctuation changes the available sites of rhetorical emphasis without automatically repairing scientific claims.
- **Evidence integrity:** a revision can have more accessible syntax while altering evidential scope, emphasis, scientific attribution, or intended contrast. Only the domain author or qualified review can resolve some of those changes.
- **Reader disagreement:** increased agreement about sentence structure does not guarantee agreement about causal interpretation or scientific significance.

## Critical asymmetry for STENDHAL

Three observed outcomes should be independently described rather than merged into “better prose”:

1. **Formal transformation:** syntax, punctuation, lexical occupancy, grammatical roles.
2. **Perceptual effect:** what becomes easy/hard to read, what appears foregrounded, what connection a reader infers.
3. **Epistemic fidelity:** whether those changes preserve, clarify, or alter the underlying researcher's claim.

Even if (1) and (2) improve, (3) may remain unknown. In science, fluent resolution of an **underspecified relationship** risks laundering a guess into apparently authoritative prose.

## Adversarial and calibration notes

- Gopen/Swan are often reduced to “shorten subject–verb spans” or “put new information last,” but their actual passage preserves **multiple** plausible rewrites. Do not cherry-pick their rules while discarding their epistemic restraint.
- The article is not proof that each Anglophone reader processes emphasis identically. Broader experimental literature must test population, genre and task.
- Some contemporary secondary takes wrongly claim these edits can always be carried out *without semantic risk*; this primary case is an explicit countercase.
- A deliberately delayed verb can successfully enact suspense, accumulated qualification or suspended judgment. Local difficulty can be intentional; the available evidence must show what was attempted and achieved.
- Cross-language application is an additional, unevaluated leap.

## Consequences for experimental design (candidate, not instruction)

Give expert and model editors the **same technical sentence without author-intent metadata**, and allow several locally clearer rewrites. Require independent notes on which claims are preserved and where uncertainty increases. Only then reveal contextual/source evidence and check factual fidelity with a qualified scientific reader.

A diagnostic that confidently calls one revision uniquely optimal before resolving authorial priorities should **fail** this test. It has mistaken a reader-interface improvement for recovered intention.
