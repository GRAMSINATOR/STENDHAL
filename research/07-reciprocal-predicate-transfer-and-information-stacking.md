# 07 — Reciprocal attribution: primitive inversion, recovered reality, emergent relations

**Date:** 2026-10-12. **Status:** operator-originated craft observation with source comparison and original probes. Provisional study, **not** a prescriptive literary rule or proof of uniform reader effects. Builds on [M02](../design/provisional-models/M02-abstraction-metabolism-and-impulse-field.md) and the [tool opportunity field](06-tool-opportunity-field.md).

## Operator's example and originating insight

> The leaves were blowing / The wind was rustling.
>
> (I invert qualifiers to get stronger effect) Here my example isn't optimal ... that lets you triple stack the information at times.

**Do not correct the operator into an adjective-only reading.** “Blowing” and “rustling” here are *predications/verbal descriptors* and their conventional relations to physical participants. The operator is discussing the broader **primitive inversion of assignments**, which can apply to adjectives, verbs, events, agent/patient roles, feelings, voices, times, or abstract situations.

In the more default event picture, **wind blows and moves the leaves, which rustle**. The crossed utterance lets **leaves blow** (which is also ordinary English for leaves moving in the wind) and **wind rustle** (possible by metonymy for the sounds it causes in vegetation) coexist. It can suggest a unified environmental event without spelling out the ordinary cause-effect chain. The two forms aren't perfect truth-conditional paraphrases; they reassign foregrounded experience. Precisely because both “leaves blowing” and “wind rustling” can be read more than one way, the example is **semantically permissive** rather than a strict permutation between two incompatible states.

## Candidate primitive (not a canonical grammar operator)

Given two entities/participants `A, B` and characteristics or predications `p, q` conventionally associated with them:

- familiar construction: `A → p ; B → q`
- exchanged construction: `A → q ; B → p`
- retained background: a reader may still reconstruct `A → p ; B → q` from conventional knowledge
- emergent interaction: the exchanged relationships can make an additional experience, atmosphere, agency shift, or contrast available.

**Danger of false precision:** this is an *illustrative schema*, not an actual semantic algebra. Inverting assignments can change who acts, what happened, how much was asserted, and even whether the situation is physically possible. It does not guarantee preserved facts or a third layer. The operator's “triple stack” is best treated as a **sometimes-achieved expressive density**:
1. conventional event/association is still activated;
2. changed text directly predicates something else;
3. interaction of conventional and novel associations yields further relational possibilities.

There may be more or fewer than three distinct, non-redundant effects, and no claim of literal information-theoretic tripling.

## A stronger independent illustration — music (synthetic)

Ordinary arrangement:

> Her hands raced over the keys. The piano trembled beneath them.

Reciprocal predicational reassignment:

> Her hands trembled over the keys. The piano raced beneath them.

This is our **original, deliberately selected construction**, not an observed human-reader result.

Here the **same two nouns** and the **same two movement predicates** remain, but their owners switch. The event remains recognisable as someone at a piano, while:
- *hands raced* foregrounds human fluency/control; *hands trembled* invites uncertainty, intensity, age, fear or embodied exertion (underdetermined without context);
- *piano trembled* suggests an instrument resonating or quivering; *piano raced* metonymically animates the instrument/music with autonomous momentum;
- the paired swap may dramatise a **tension between the player's bodily control and the music's onward motion**. That is a contextual interpretive hypothesis, not an intrinsic guarantee of better style;
- the second version may change the *implied performance facts*, so it would not be a faithful rewrite if the original required steady hands.

Notice how little sophisticated planning is needed to *generate* the novel relation: two predicates are simply exchanged. Whether the passage works in its intended artistic context is an independent craft question.

## Three genuinely different transformations, too easily called “inversion”

**S1 — transferred modifier / epithet**: attach an attribute conventionally belonging to A onto a related B (e.g. a person's anxiety becomes the room's “nervous” atmosphere). This includes many instances of hypallage but does not exhaust the user's method.

**S2 — reciprocal predicate reassignment:** two participants exchange their characteristic actions or qualities, as in the piano/hands or leaves/wind exercises. This can preserve the subject pair while changing agency, rhythm and animacy.

**S3 — causal / experiential rotation:** instead of swapping words exactly, rotate *which participant is treated as the source, carrier or observer* of an event: the leaves make the wind audible; sound is ascribed to wind instead of leaves. This can produce multiple mutually reinforcing descriptions without a strict word permutation.

Other kinds: role inversion (victim/pursuer), temporal reversal (consequence precedes presented cause), frame inversion (background becomes foreground), polarity inversion (certainty/doubt), evaluator inversion (narrator judgement assigned to character). The point is the **same deeply assimilated simple abstraction**, not a requirement to classify dozens of literary devices first.

## Classical source and conceptual limits

**French academy:** *Dictionnaire de l'Académie française*, 9th ed., “Hypallage”, https://www.dictionnaire-academie.fr/article/A9H1279 . Its example from Virgil of exchanged darkness/solitude is explicitly **double hypallage**, an unusually close literary comparison for reciprocal adjective assignment. This is a *specific historical figure*, not proof that the operator's general transformer must be restricted to epithet exchanges.

**Cognitive linguistics:** Klaus-Uwe Panther and Linda L. Thornburg (2023), “Hypallage is a rare bird. Not.”, *Lingua* 289, 103511, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lingua.2023.103511 . Their accessible abstract and article preview propose that hypallactic transfer appears well beyond literary adjectives, including clausal and speech-act constructions, and that shared conceptual frames and inference permit efficient comprehension. **Evidence status:** C1 publisher abstract/preview; full scholarly paper not independently inspected.

**Translation-sensitive comparison:** Agnieszka Kulczyńska (2018), “Hypallage or transferred attribute in the Jerzy Andrzejewski's novel ‘Wielki Tydzień’ and in its French version”, https://lsmll.journals.umcs.pl/lsmll/article/view/7667 . Abstract screened only (C1). Its concern with unusual collocations and how they travel across languages recommends testing transfer robustness, not universally equating Polish/French/English results.

**More general source caution:** Leona Toker's 2015 discussion of hypallage in Dickens links transferred attributes with metaphor, metonymy and personification (scholarly abstract, C1), https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/hypallage-and-the-literalization-of-metaphors-in-a-dickens-text/ . Different theoretical traditions need not describe the same microscopic operation identically.

## A negative control

> The clock ticked. The room waited.

vs.

> The clock waited. The room ticked.

The second may evoke an uncanny, almost physical time experience, *but* it also introduces the possibility that the clock stopped or time has acquired agency. That can be exactly right in a story, or pretentious/incoherent. The reader may not recover the same temporal facts. **An inversion does not imply preservation**.

A deliberately weak case:

> The engine coughed. The old man wheezed.
>
> The engine wheezed. The old man coughed.

Both predicates are already naturally available to both subjects. The reciprocal exchange may produce little additional semantic tension. **Simple novelty of assignment is not guaranteed, even though an operator was applied.** A comparative test should sample cases like this, not only polished successful swaps.

## Why it matters to abstraction metabolism

The operator knew how to invert relationships at a root level and **transferred that abstraction to predicational ownership**. Neither the ability to apply it nor the resulting new possibility depends on knowing the technical term *hypallage*, or on lengthy expert planning. This is a clean concrete instance of the proposed M02 distinction:
- generativity of the primitive;
- sophistication of the substrate or operator chain;
- aesthetic quality and meaning of outcome;
- later selection and rewriting.

Not every stylistic density gain has to be pre-planned. If the speaker discovers a convincing new pairing through careless swapping, that is a real generative outcome. The writing can then alter further intentions.
