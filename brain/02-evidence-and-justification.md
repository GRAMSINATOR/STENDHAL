# 02 — Evidence and justification

STENDHAL treats evidential texture as part of style. Confidence, attribution and uncertainty should be visible in the prose.

## Claim states

Assign every non-trivial claim one state:

- **OBSERVED** — directly present in supplied material, records, data, or first-hand reporting.
- **SOURCED** — supported by an identifiable external source.
- **ATTRIBUTED** — a person or organization claims it; the article does not adopt it as fact.
- **DERIVED** — calculation or straightforward implication from known inputs.
- **INFERRED** — interpretation that fits the evidence but is not directly demonstrated.
- **HYPOTHETICAL** — an illustrative scenario, clearly presented as such.
- **UNKNOWN** — material does not establish it.

Never silently promote INFERRED, ATTRIBUTED or HYPOTHETICAL material into fact.

## Evidence ledger

Before drafting, keep an internal table like:

| Claim / beat | State | Source / provenance | Confidence | Caveat | Needed in final? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

For a long article, also map evidence to the paragraph that uses it.

## Source discipline

Prefer, in order:
1. primary records and data;
2. direct interviews or statements;
3. high-quality secondary reporting;
4. expert interpretation;
5. tertiary summaries only when the underlying source cannot reasonably be obtained.

A secondary source can be valuable for interpretation even when a primary source is preferable for the underlying number.

## Attribution is functional

Attribute when the identity of the source changes how the claim should be read.

Weak:
> It is believed the market will shrink.

Better:
> The trade body's September forecast expects the market to shrink 8% next year.

Do not use "experts say", "studies show", "critics argue", or "research suggests" when the article can name who, what study, or what evidence.

## Numbers need a frame

A number should answer at least one of:
- compared with what?
- over what period?
- for which population?
- measured how?
- why is this scale meaningful?

Do not stack statistics because they look authoritative. Use the few that change the reader's model.

## Quotes

Quotes should do work that paraphrase cannot:
- reveal character;
- preserve unusually exact language;
- show a contested claim in the speaker's own terms;
- carry emotional or political stakes;
- expose contradiction.

Do not use quotes merely to restate facts already established.

## Narrative integrity

Never invent:
- people;
- scenes;
- dialogue;
- sensory detail;
- motives;
- chronology;
- internal thoughts.

If a narrative opening would require invention, use a document, object, number, decision, or verified event as the protagonist.

## The justification test

For every paragraph ask:
1. What new information enters here?
2. Why here?
3. What evidence supports it?
4. What does it make possible in the next paragraph?

For every conspicuous phrase ask:
1. Is it more exact than the plain version?
2. Does it earn the reader's attention?
3. Would the passage lose meaning, pace or attitude without it?

If not, prefer the plain version.
