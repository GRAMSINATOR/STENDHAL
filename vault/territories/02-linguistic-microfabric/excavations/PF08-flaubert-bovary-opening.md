# PF08 — Flaubert: when a name turns into sound, and a cap governs syntax

**Status:** targeted close reading in the **French original** of *Madame Bovary*, Part I Chapter I, through the classroom scene and shift into family background. Original-language sounds are not interchangeable with English translations.

## Small-scale traces: check the primary text before naming a device

01. **'Nous étions'** occupies the subject position at the opening. The initial witness is collective, not an identified narrator with an individual name.
02. **'Un nouveau' / 'le nouveau'** nominalises the adjective *nouveau*, withholding the schoolboy's proper name. Tanner explicitly examines this designation and its social context; his larger account of novelty is an interpretation.
03. **First sentence with layered attachments:** principal arrival, followed by additional persons and objects. This is visible syntactic sequencing; an inferred reader's visual attention is not measured.
04. **Sleep and performance:** students who were asleep rise *comme surpris dans son travail*. The *comme* differentiates seeming industriousness from demonstrated industry.
05. **School authority speaks in conditional/administrative language:** the Proviseur's recommendation of placement and advancement contrasts lexically with the new boy's later speech failure.
06. **Items of clothing and materials** (jacket, cuffs, socks, shoes, nails) are not incidental abstract traits: the character is rendered through objects and fit, with period social vocabulary.
07. **The cap custom:** schoolboys habitually throw caps on the floor; the newcomer does not. A norm is conveyed by practice and an exception rather than by an abstract precept.
08. **Extended cap description:** layered geometrical and textural nouns (including *boudins*, *losanges*, *velours*, *soutache*, *croisillon*) break down one object into sequences of shapes. The description has a different density from the following brief actions.
09. **Two-short-clause action:** *Il se leva; sa casquette tomba.* A semicolon separates subject-predicate events. The next independent sentence records collective laughter. Compare syntax directly rather than assuming brevity always works.
10. **Repeated attempts with altered agency:** he retrieves the cap, then a neighbour knocks it off, then he retrieves it again. Repetition is relationally different on each occasion.
11. **Name withheld and then phonically disturbed:** the name first emerges as unintelligible murmuring. The boy then produces *Charbovari*; classmates repeat that string, and the teacher later acquires the standard proper name *Charles Bovary*. The loss of phonetic clarity is explicit; a precise accent-dependent account is still unavailable.
12. **A long sound-event sentence:** the uproar leaps, rises in *crescendo*, rolls into isolated *notes*, subsides and restarts. Temporal acoustic progression is expressed by a chain of successive verbs, rather than by generic adjective 'loud'.
13. **The cry becomes a distributed social event:** *Charbovari* is repeated by several students, and the reported sound recurs in different regions of the room. A proper name has been temporarily converted into a publicly shared graphic/sonic object.
14. **Later truncated *Ma cas…*:** the attempt to ask about the cap is visibly incomplete, then met by the teacher's authoritative speech. Its fragment links lexical material with the earlier extended cap episode.
15. **Assessment of *tournures*:** Charles's schoolwork is described as acceptable in grammatical rules but lacking elegance in turns of expression. This is a school assessment *inside the story*, not evidence Flaubert inserted a manifesto about his prose.
16. **Collective observer reappears:** *Nous le vîmes* explicitly returns after the riot. Subsequently the narrative remarks that no one among the 'us' can now remember him. It would be incorrect to say the narrative 'we' vanishes immediately after the first paragraph.
17. **Time/knowledge boundary:** extensive biographical history follows, exceeding what the first-person school group has explicitly been shown witnessing. Whether this is narrative inconsistency, purposeful transition or another mechanism is the work of criticism, not an immediate fact about prose quality.

## Comparative critical note

Tony Tanner's close attention to the category *nouveau* and collective *nous* is grounded in actual language. But treating the new boy as a philosophical embodiment of an abstract *newness* is a distinct critical layer. A critic may perceive all the same actual words yet decline that expansion.
