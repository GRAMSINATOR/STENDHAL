# GR04 — The discarded draft as a transformation of the writer

**Origin:** STENDHAL operator observation, 2026-10-12. **Epistemic status:** a precise practitioner hypothesis, not an established cognitive invariant or a documented historical author method.

## The proposed experience, preserved without reinterpretative inflation

An author writes a substantial version, judges its sentences not to have found the desired expression, **removes the text entirely from view**, and composes again without looking at it or copying local wording. The prior attempt is not the literal reference text for a normal edit. Nonetheless, the first act of writing has changed the writer's understanding, internal orientation, possibilities and sensitivities. The second attempt may reproduce neither the structure nor the wording, yet carry something shaped by the discarded attempt.

The crucial proposed causal location is **the effect of producing the draft on the composing agent**, not transfer of strings from draft A to draft B.

This is importantly different from:
1. local in-situ polishing;
2. linear paraphrase of visible draft;
3. copyedit under an unchanged plan;
4. asking an LLM to 'rewrite this better' while all previous wording remains on its context;
5. recovering memorised original wording verbatim.

The writer need not necessarily undertake this consciously, and fast skilled writing may already incorporate procedures acquired through long training. No universal claim about how many full rewrites great writers require follows.

## Research proximity: three different scientific descriptions

### 1. Writing as knowledge transforming

Carl Bereiter and Marlene Scardamalia, *The Psychology of Written Composition* (1987), ERIC book record: https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED279025

The book distinguishes knowledge telling from the more complex, rhetorical problem-solving activity of skilled writing. Its chapter outline and abstract are verifiable, but we have **not** read the whole book in this acquisition pass.

### 2. Writing as knowledge constituting

David Galbraith, *Writing as Discovery* (2009), University of Southampton archival record: https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/337490/

The author argues that implicit text-production processes can constitute new understanding rather than merely translate already known content into language. This is much closer to the operator's assertion that writing produces a new cognitive starting state. **It does not directly establish that deleting the entire first draft is necessary or beneficial.**

### 3. Measured relationship of spontaneous production, revision, and understanding

Veerle Baaijen and David Galbraith, *Discovery Through Writing: Relationships with Writing Processes and Text Quality*, *Cognition and Instruction* 36(3), 2018, DOI https://doi.org/10.1080/07370008.2018.1456431 ; archived text https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/421401/

The paper uses keystroke logging with contrasting planning conditions, and reports that spontaneous sentence production and global revision have distinctive/opposing associations with discovery and text quality. It argues that both implicit generative processes and explicit reorganisation contribute. **This is not a direct experiment comparing discarding an entire first draft with revising it visibly.**

Linda Flower and John Hayes, *A Cognitive Process Theory of Writing* (1981), https://doi.org/10.58680/ccc198115885, provides an earlier account of planning, formulation and reviewing as recursive rather than mechanically sequential; we have a bibliographic paper record and secondary overview, not direct full-paper close reading.

## Important alternative explanations to distinguish

- **Internalisation:** first production changes the conceptual content or goals subsequently available to a writer.
- **Filtering:** writing exposes what feels wrong, allowing unwanted directions to be abandoned.
- **De-anchoring:** hiding the draft removes its phrasing from immediate perception, reducing attraction to its local structure.
- **Retrieval:** memory privileges some relations and not others; the second text may reflect selectively reconstructed content.
- **Practice/exposure:** any second attempt benefits from having spent additional time on the problem.
- **Task regeneration:** simply asking again, without keeping the original, can lead to a different sample even if no beneficial cognitive transformation occurred.
- **Evaluation artifacts:** a judge may prefer different prose simply because it is less similar, not because it is more skilful.

These are **candidate explanations for experiment design, not causal facts** about the operator's experience.

## Why this becomes technically difficult with LLMs

Human writing and the act of forgetting/unseeing a draft are not mirrored by a second completion in the *same conversation context*. If prior generated words remain visible, the second process can rely on local lexical priming and paraphrase. If they are completely withheld from a fresh model call, the fresh call also **does not inherit an episodic memory of having written them**. We must not call this 'real forgetting' or pretend there is a retained internal lived trace.

A plausible external harness experiment can transfer only a **bounded, inspectable state artifact** produced by the first process: perhaps source facts, discoveries, current intentions, rejected assumptions, emotional/experiential targets and open uncertainties, without including the surface prose. That is an engineered informational bottleneck, **not a replica of human tacit memory**.

Crucially the residue might be distorted if over-formalised. Test more than one transfer medium (minimal note, critical report, semantic field, abstract-free memory-like recall, no transfer).

## Follow-up research tasks

1. Locate credible first-person examples of accomplished writers actually discarding complete drafts and rewriting without consulting them. University radical-revision instructions document the *practice* in some educational contexts, but are not evidence for the exact mechanism. See CUNY/Baruch resource https://blogs.baruch.cuny.edu/writingcenter/strategies-for-radical-revision/ and UW-Madison account https://writing.wisc.edu/handbook/processandstructure/revisinglongprojects/.
2. Distinguish global conceptual learning from phonemic/syntactic craft effects: the hypothesis concerns both, but they may not improve together.
3. Compare genuine LLM isolation/transfer regimes; a mere 'forget the previous text' instruction in the same prompt is a weaker experimental intervention.
4. Do not elevate erase-and-rewrite to a standard recipe until distinct writing regimes and failure cases have been observed.

## Research status

**STENDHAL hypothesis registered.** Direct external confirmation: related cognitive models and studies, *not* the precise erasure effect. No controlled experiment performed.
