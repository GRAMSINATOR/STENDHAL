# EXP-009 — Cheap inversion / reciprocal attribution / expressive density

**Date:** 2026-10-12. **Status:** test design and openly authored pilot cases; no reader trials or independent model comparisons yet.

**Input insight:** operator regularly inverts descriptive predicates between related participants to sometimes **stack multiple pieces of information** in a short span. Example: “The leaves were blowing / The wind was rustling.” See the [closely reasoned research note](../../research/07-reciprocal-predicate-transfer-and-information-stacking.md) and [M02 primitive assimilation model](../../design/provisional-models/M02-abstraction-metabolism-and-impulse-field.md).

## The genuine claim at risk

The simplest permutation of two predicates can sometimes generate an unexpectedly high number of *relevant simultaneously available readings*, while leaving the conventional underlying event recoverable. A more elaborate planning machine is not a prerequisite for that novel structure.

This is **not** a claim that:
- any swap is an improvement;
- language literally gains 3x Shannon information;
- original facts are always preserved;
- the historical term “hypallage” describes all transferred agency and predicate cases;
- the originator was conscious of every effect.

## Working pair; openly synthetic

**S0 / familiar:**
> Her hands raced over the keys. The piano trembled beneath them.

**S1 / full reciprocal switch:**
> Her hands trembled over the keys. The piano raced beneath them.

The stable nouns and verb lemmas make the surface change inspectable. This is not a true controlled semantic minimal pair, because the change **may alter what is happening**: the performer's steadiness and the piano/music's apparent agency. That very risk is part of the intended assessment.

**S2 / partial switch:**
> Her hands trembled over the keys. The piano trembled beneath them.

A common bodily quality spreads to both participants. This is NOT a simple inverted mapping; it's an experimental comparison for **contagion/saturation**.

**S3 / cross-causal reinterpretation:**
> Her hands trembled over the keys; the piano seemed to hurry them along.

This modifies more than predicates: apparent agency passes from person to instrument. It may create a vivid subjective relation, but adds explicit interpretive wording and is a worse strict test of permutation.

**S4 / semantically flat contrast:**
> The engine coughed. The old man wheezed.

vs.
> The engine wheezed. The old man coughed.

This may scarcely change interpretation because both predicates are highly conventional for both subjects. Essential negative control against claiming rich stacking everywhere.

**S5 / strong semantic drift:**
> The clock ticked. The room waited.

vs.
> The clock waited. The room ticked.

The second can imply a stopped clock or animate room. It may be a successful speculative passage but **not a preserved-world rewrite**.

## Dimensions to probe—not a quality formula

| Observation | Question | Evidence type |
|---|---|---|
| Surface | What exact predicate or attachment moved? | deterministic diff and grammar |
| Conventional associations | Do readers still access the prior wind-leaves or hands-piano relationships? | task-based reception; not assumed |
| Additional relation | Is there an intelligible *new* attribution, agency relation or atmosphere? | qualified close reading; reader reports |
| Literal commitments | Did the situation become materially different or contradictory? | semantic audit |
| Sonority | Did the swapped word order alter stress, local recurrence or rhythm? | text/phonological analysis, audio if needed |
| Emphasis and time | What is foregrounded first and what reading takes shape when? | reader-focused hypothesis |
| Contextual fit | Does the new version serve this literary environment? | plural editorial judgment |
| Novelty vs sophistication | Was the operator merely elementary while the resulting structure became complex? | operator provenance, different assessments |

A critic should be allowed to say “this is both a meaningful new possibility and a bad sentence.”

## Testing procedure (unrun)

1. Assemble **20 paired-source events**; 10 natural/causally close (wind/leaves, piano/hands, rain/window, voice/room) and 10 semantically distant, plus 5 non-fiction fact-bound briefs.
2. Blindly draw one of four conditions: no change, one attribution switch, full reciprocal switch, or unconstrained paraphrase. Equalise generation budgets.
3. Log exact diff. Ask independent human or model graders to mark the **literal event**, **background event recovered**, **additional plausible relation**, and any **overclaim or causal loss**, separately.
4. Add 1–2 paragraphs of surrounding story to half the samples. See whether context turns a swap from false cleverness into a functional meaning, or vice versa.
5. Conduct French and English versions without asserting translations preserve exact connotations or phonetic texture.
6. Do at least some **listener** comparisons for passage rhythm; sound texture is not reducible to word meaning.
7. Test whether the second-stage writer chooses to keep an awkward but fertile inversion, revise it or discard it—without assuming more editing means more creative ability.
8. Preserve all failed examples and unexpected no-op results, particularly when predicates are both ordinary for both nouns.

## A sharper generative search

The important experiment is not “prompt an LLM to write hypallage.” It is:

- retrieve 2–3 participants that already belong to one event or scene;
- select one primitive: **INVERT**;
- pick *any* relation (verb, quality, power, viewpoint, temporality);
- run the reassignment, even if it initially looks silly;
- compare what is actually present with what the original context already supports;
- **optionally** use more complex passes to explore a promising accident.

If this works, a **primitive abstraction applied to a carefully noticed substrate** can unlock depth without a giant rhetorical technique library.

## Failure policy

- If a swap only yields exotic noise, do not invent a literary interpretation to rescue it.
- If common verb associations make both versions interchangeable, record a creative near-no-op.
- If a shift introduces a new factual claim in nonfiction, flag loss, not stylistic superiority.
- If a reader reconstructs three incompatible pictures rather than enriched coexistence, don't call it “three layers”.
- If a single uninterrupted writer already does this well without prompts, our tool should learn to notice, not enforce the operation.
