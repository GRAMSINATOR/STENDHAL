# PR01 — *King Lear*, Tolstoy, Orwell: passage-grounded triangulation

**Status: first primary-text comparison, 2026-10-12.** Four loci examined directly. Original work + two accomplished hostile/favourable readers. No techniques inferred as authorial intention.

- Leo Tolstoy, *Tolstoy on Shakespeare: A Critical Essay on Shakespeare*, 1906, translated by V. Tchertkoff and I. F. Mayo; Part I especially sections II–V. https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/27726/pg27726-images.html

## Locus 1 — Does Lear have any motive to surrender power?

**Text:** *Lear* 1.1, Folger lines 37–58, especially 41–44 and 54–55. In his opening instructions Lear declares that he has divided his territory, wants to be free of the business of age, and seeks to transfer rule. This is before the daughters' declarations. Later in 1.1, lines 148–155 he specifies alternating residences, the retinue of 100 knights, and retention of royal title while relinquishing the actual functions of rule.

**Tolstoy:** Part I §IV asserts that Lear has no *necessity or motive* for abdication, and condemns the premise as arbitrary.

**Orwell:** directly disputes the allegation; says the desire to retire from state cares is already explained in the opening.

**Evidence adjudication:**
- On the **literal textual question** "is a reason stated?", the play supplies one. Tolstoy's unqualified absence claim is textually refutable.
- On the **dramaturgical question** "is the described reason sufficient to make Lear's conduct believable?", the question remains open. Tolstoy can have a coherent objection about disproportion and credibility that survives correction of the literal claim.
- The dialogue establishes a peculiar allocation of ceremonial status and practical control. This is observed; the further dramatic effect is interpretive, not established by mere presence.

**Facet documented:** hostile criticism may conflate the presence of a stated motive with its psychological adequacy. Keep both questions distinct.

## Locus 2 — The Fool's crown/egg exchange

**Text:** *Lear* 1.4, Folger lines approximately 716–759. Lear asks whether he is the fool; the Fool redirects the title toward Lear. In the following exchange, an egg split into two 'crowns' becomes the figure for a royal crown divided in two. The Fool then distinguishes Lear's bald head from the golden headgear and sings about wise people behaving foolishly. Shortly after, he describes Lear as making daughters into mothers; the reversal is explicit in Folger lines 752–754.

**Tolstoy:** §II describes this and neighbouring Fool exchanges as prolonged, witless, and unrelated or ill-suited to the situation; an unsuccessful attempted comic effect.

**Orwell:** regards the Fool as the play's persistent, sharp commentary on Lear's folly, including its melancholy, and says the Fool's presence is integral rather than intrusive. See paragraphs on the Fool midway through his essay.

**Directly observable textual relation:** the quibbles repeatedly return to Lear's *specific earlier allocation of kingdom and authority*. Consequently, describing the whole exchange as **unrelated to the situation** misses a local semantic linkage. This observation does not prove that the jokes are enjoyable, well paced or necessary at the length printed.

**Facet documented:** a device can be thematically and referentially connected yet judged unfunny, overextended, or tonally disruptive. Relevance and aesthetic success are not synonyms.

## Locus 3 — Storm, hyperbolic language, and survival of images

**Text:** *Lear* 3.2, Folger lines 1776–1815. Lear orders elements to overwhelm the world, then momentarily distinguishes the impersonal elements from his daughters. The Fool immediately contrasts spectacular cosmic rage with a dry house and the present need for shelter; his later verse switches register again. Kent then enters and argues from the material danger of weather to human bodies.

**Tolstoy:** §II describes Lear's speeches as pompous attempts to express despair, the Fool as senseless, and the storm as an extravagant effect undermining conviction.

**Orwell:** says the arrangement of storm, old man, Fool, loss and suffering retains a kind of poetry as pattern/atmosphere even apart from verbal music; nevertheless he calls the play sprawling and concedes excess.

**Primary scene observations:**
- The modes of address are different: Lear apostrophises wind, fire and thunder; Fool addresses 'nuncle' and negotiates domestic shelter; Kent addresses the danger of remaining outdoors.
- Lear's references to daughters/kingdom connect the cosmic speech to domestic and political events; the Fool's interruptions re-ground the scene in shelter, hierarchy and bodily consequences.
- A question about whether the storm is a naturalistic event, externalisation, ritual spectacle or theatrical exaggeration cannot be settled by listing its vocabulary.

**Facet documented:** one scene contains multiple co-present kinds of rhetoric and scale movement. The reader may regard the coexistence as effective counterpoint or stylistic excess. Neither judgment is a bare linguistic fact.

## Locus 4 — Does Shakespeare give everyone the same voice?

**Tolstoy:** §IV's more general charge is that Shakespeare lacks individualised speech and gives people a similar elevated, theatrical language.

**Specific text counterpressure:** Compare the adjacent Folger voices in 3.2: Lear's vocative, apocalyptic imperatives; Fool's proverbial/domestic and sometimes obscene comedy; Kent's concrete caution about the storm. At least locally, the claim that these speakers are indistinguishable **in all registers** is untenable.

**What remains unresolved:** The different voices can share an underlying Shakespearean rhetorical signature, as Tolstoy claims, even while differing in register and aim. A serious general test requires a broader stratified sample of scenes and authors; three speeches do not refute a corpus-wide claim.

**Facet documented:** speaker-specific differentiation, shared authorial idiom and perceived naturalness are distinct properties.

## The deeper critic-vs-critic dispute

Tolstoy prizes credible action, character-appropriate language, emotional sincerity and moral intelligibility; he explicitly treats much of Shakespeare's spectacle as counterfeit.

Orwell insists that poetry, arrangement, image, tonal counterpoint and tragedy cannot simply be erased by paraphrasing the work as implausible events. Yet his response contains **his own** speculative accounts of Tolstoy's motives; those should be separately interrogated rather than adopted as psychological evidence.

Neither critic can be reduced to 'pro-clarity' or 'pro-obscurity'.

## What this comparison does not establish

- Not a verdict on which author is ultimately right.
- Not a proof that Shakespeare consciously planned all observable interactions.
- Not an exhaustive close reading of *Lear*.
- Not a defensible quantitative count of techniques.
- Not yet a study of staged delivery, actor prosody, historical audience or original folio/quarto textual variance.
- Not a license to convert observed literary devices into prompt-writing rules.
