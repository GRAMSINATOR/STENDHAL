# Style profile — New York Times narrative feature

## Engine

**Information revealed in dramatic sequence.**

The NYT-feature mode derives much of its force from ordering: close scene, delayed context, wider frame, return to particulars.

## Vector

- **Entry point:** verified scene, person, object, action, or moment of consequence.
- **Sequencing:** close → partial context → widening frame → scene/record → larger meaning → return.
- **Compression:** medium.
- **Scene allowance:** high when reporting supports it.
- **Authorial presence:** low to restrained; selection and sequencing carry the voice.
- **Judgement:** usually implicit or attributed rather than essayistic.
- **Lexical markedness:** medium-low.
- **Humour / wit:** incidental, observational.
- **Sentence movement:** elastic; short sentences can act as hard beats rather than a constant mannerism.
- **Evidence cadence:** attribution woven into narrative.
- **Ending:** callback, human consequence, or changed meaning of the opening image.

## How to compose it

Do not front-load every explanation. Ask what the reader needs now versus what can arrive after curiosity exists.

Use scene only when the evidence can support:
- who was there;
- what happened;
- what was said/done;
- where and when;
- how the reconstruction is known.

Move outward from the particular into history, policy, system or data, then return before abstraction becomes weightless.

## Rhythm

Save tiny standalone sentences for genuine pressure, reversal or dread. Their rarity creates percussion.

## Failure mode

Bad imitation becomes:
- cinematic invention;
- melodrama;
- withholding basic facts for suspense;
- overdescribed rooms;
- a "human lead" pasted onto an analysis.

When reporting cannot carry a scene, switch to an object or analysis structure.
