# GR07 — An acoustic referent is not the phonetic fabric of its word

**Source-led microcase** accompanying [Flaubert GR05](GR05-flaubert-rough-draft-variants.md). **2026-10-12**.

## Attested editorial variation

Flaubert's rough witnesses of the opening to *L'Éducation sentimentale* include the noun **sifflement** for the steam's sound, while the corrected autograph and published reading use **bruissement**.

## A crucial distinction for STENDHAL's phonics research

There are **at least two separable sound questions**:

**1. What kind of sound does the noun refer to?** French historical lexicography confirms contrasting conventional senses:
- The Dictionnaire de l'Académie, *sifflement*, already in the 1718 edition, discusses whistling, sharp sounds of wind, arrows and other high-frequency noise. https://www.dictionnaire-academie.fr/article/A2S0531
- *Bruissement*, in the 1762 fourth edition, is characterised as an indistinct/confused sound (also waves/ears). https://www.dictionnaire-academie.fr/article/A4B1311

This is lexical **semantics of described sound**. It is not direct evidence for how a reader hears the sound of *the words*.

**2. What sounds does the utterance itself contain?** The phonemes, articulation and timing of /.../ at that position in *le [noun] de la vapeur*, possible neighbouring consonance, stress, schwa realisation, fluency of clause boundaries, cadence and silent-reading phonological effects require a separate, accent/period-specific phonological investigation. Do not equate the noun meaning 'whistle' with an onomatopoeic sound effect that must be perceived by a reader.

**3. And what about the surrounding syntax?** Rough *disparaissait sous* versus final *s'absorbait dans* changes grammatical relations between noises; even a lexically sensible substitution cannot be judged in isolation.

## What we can say now

We have an actual historically attested selection among words whose **meaning is acoustic**, located within a clause about competing noises, and accompanied by transformations in the verb and preposition.

## What we cannot say now

We do not have proof that Flaubert preferred the *phonetic texture* of the chosen word. His decision may involve referential precision, material imagery, sentence timing, cultural associations or more than one factor. Nor do dictionaries measure how historical or modern readers respond to a phrase.

## Next excavation

A linguist/phoneticist could annotate several defensible pronunciations and compare 1) whole-phrase phoneme sequences, 2) lexical denotation, 3) rhythms under oral reading, 4) differences among readers/listeners. STENDHAL should test whether its own perception distinguishes these dimensions **before** drawing any further aesthetic inference.

This case is small but methodologically important: under-indexing of phonology could persist even when a model talks confidently about 'whistling' and 'rustling' as semantic concepts.
