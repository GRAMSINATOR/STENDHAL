# W08-03 — Oral composition: formula, parallelism, body and audience

**Source statuses:** original fieldwork-based monographs/chapters and academic abstracts screened, with publisher-accessible arguments. No raw recording personally transcribed in this tranche.

## The Parry–Lord tradition: formulae in metre

Albert B. Lord, *The Singer of Tales* (1960), Harvard Center for Hellenic Studies open edition: https://chs.harvard.edu/book/lord-albert-bates-the-singer-of-tales/ .

The Parry–Lord oral-formulaic account develops from field recordings of South Slavic oral epic performers and analysis of Homeric language. It identifies recurrent phrase units associated with a **metrically conditioned expressive need**, describing singers who actively compose or recombine material in performance instead of simply reciting memorised fixed text.

**Access status:** verified scholarly overview and edition, not independent field-recording audit. The original record of an oral singer may still involve transcription, translation and editorial decisions.

## A highly qualified and actually articulated objection

James J. Fox, *Master Poets, Ritual Masters*, Australian National University Press open text, preface https://press-files.anu.edu.au/downloads/press/p337693/html/preface.xhtml?page=6&referer= .

Fox explicitly identifies a limitation of a metrical formula as the default organising concept: a substantial range of oral traditions organises composition through **canonical parallelism**, with structural correspondence rather than recurring metrically matched units as the decisive property.

**This is the kind of documented expert-level technical disagreement we seek.** It concerns the very units by which repeated expression is made, not a ranking of oral cultures.

**Qualification:** Fox is not claiming that Lord's historical South Slavic observations never occurred; he contests whether their central definitions generalise to the different traditions he investigates. Preserve both findings rather than forcing a winner.

## Ruth Finnegan: field research and situated forms

Ruth Finnegan, *Oral Literature in Africa*, 2012 open edition (original 1970): https://www.openbookpublishers.com/books/10.11647/obp.0025 ; newer chapter *Composition* (2025) https://www.openbookpublishers.com/books/10.11647/obp.0428/chapters/10.11647/obp.0428.03 .

Her research documents oral forms across distinct African societies, including performance, audience, occasions, proverb/song/drum-language practices, and the interplay of memorisation and new composition. The 2025 chapter explicitly examines strengths and limitations of a single oral-formulaic explanation in a range of traditions.

**Important:** These cannot be reduced to 'non-Western writers improvise'. Traditions differ and fieldworker's categories are not the artist's own complete theory.

## Further sensory dimension — the audience's eyes

A 2021 scholarly article, *Ojú l’ọ̀rọ̀ọ́ wà: The Praise Gaze in Oríkì Performance*, describes participant observation and audiovisual analysis of **Yorùbá praise-poetry performance**, with attention to gaze not represented in ordinary textual transcription: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/18125980.2021.2015247 .

**Evidence level:** abstract and reported method only, no performance videos personally reviewed. The possible interaction among words, voice, gaze and audience is a testable domain, not a verified result about all oríkì events.

## What remains open

- Need comparable original performance recordings from Lord's archive and Fox's examples.
- Must distinguish formula recurrence, canonical parallelism, vocal rhythm and actual corporeal/audience exchange.
- Need counterexamples of exceptional performance whose surface is deliberately irregular, silent or fractured.
- A text generator that only sees transcripts is not demonstrably perceiving everything a performer constructs.
- We should not conclude written craft is inferior or that oral performance is all improvisation.

**Next:** annotate two actual published transcribed performances alongside recordings, precisely marking repeated language and changing occasions; recruit original-language specialists.
