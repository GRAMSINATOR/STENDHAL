# PF11 — Austen's *Emma*: a one-word difference that must not become invented craft history

**2026-10-12.** **Evidence status:** C2 direct collation of online text witnesses; C0 for first-edition *reading of the disputed word itself*. An 1816 first-edition **facsimile has been located**, but the relevant scanned page has **not yet been inspected**. This is a *witness-control dossier*, not evidence Austen personally revised the pronoun.

## Identical passage, different visible text

Target: *Emma*, volume III, chapter VII (modern chapter 43), immediately after Knightley's rebuke and Emma's departure by carriage. This is the deeply felt transition from reported shame to unquoted self-accusation.

- Wikisource https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Emma_(Austen)/Volume_3/Chapter_7 shows **“The truth of his representation”**, and a dash after one of the rhetorical questions.
- Project Gutenberg https://www.gutenberg.org/files/158/158-h/158-h.htm , SparkNotes full text https://www.sparknotes.com/lit/emma/full-text/volume-iii-chapter-vii/ and University of Nebraska Austen database https://austen.unl.edu/search?page=4&rows=50&sex=&speaker_name=%22Almost+everybody%22 show **“The truth of this representation”**, with varying punctuation.
- British-Library-derived educational commentary by Kathryn Sutherland, reproduced at https://www.britlit.org/pub/9jxum9pk/release/1 , quotes the **“his”** witness. This independently establishes editorial circulation, **not** first-edition correctness.

## A meaningful semantic/rhetorical possibility — not yet a historical fact

**his representation** explicitly points backward to Knightley's ownership of the accusation and leaves his judgment sharply present at the moment Emma internalises it.

**this representation** is demonstrative/anaphoric: a claim or moral image just invoked is held up in the sentence, without explicitly re-naming whose it was. Its deictic force could alter the immediate centre of attention.

These are *legitimate close-reading consequences of the two strings.* But **we cannot attribute the contrast to Austen's intention or a draft revision**, because the variants may result from later editorial decisions, transcription errors or copy-text differences. A critic who builds an argument about Austen's exact pronoun without consulting a first edition is making a provenance error.

## The source-critical failure mode

1. Two web transcriptions disagree on one word and punctuation.
2. Both could sustain compelling stylistic explanations.
3. Neither stylistic story establishes an authorial choice until early witnesses/edition editorial histories are checked.
4. If a model silently selects whichever word best fits its preferred theory, it has **manufactured authorial intention from source noise**.
