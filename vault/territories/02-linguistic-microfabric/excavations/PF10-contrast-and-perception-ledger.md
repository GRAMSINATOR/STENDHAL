# PF10 — Microscopic prose: an observation ledger with open interpretations

**Status:** comparative reading record, not a universal representation or generative schema. Pair the two detailed primary-source close readings: [Austen](PF07-austen-emma-opening.md), [Flaubert](PF08-flaubert-bovary-opening.md). First establish *what is present*; a possible literary effect does not become a fact merely because an accomplished critic can describe it.

## Contrast 1 — Which words temporarily withhold what the reader can know?

| Textual feature | Verifiable property | Reading to investigate |
|---|---|---|
| Austen's *seemed* | Finite predicate qualifies the apparent possession of advantages | Who supplies the evaluative perspective, and when does the qualification become salient? |
| Flaubert's *le nouveau* | Substantivised adjective in place of an individual's proper name | Does the generic label affect how collective classmates perceive him? |
| Austen's *real evils* | Direct naming of downsides after several qualified favourable descriptions | How does overt judgment retrospectively alter earlier wording? |
| Flaubert's *Charbovari* | Nonstandard utterance repeated by the class before the properly spelled name emerges | How does a name shift from identity information to social sound material? |

**Observation:** these are different linguistic constructions at different scales. Do not homogenise them into a "withhold then reveal" template.

## Contrast 2 — How far does a punctuation mark reach?

- Austen's **first-opening semicolon** joins two qualified descriptions and is evidenced in multiple witnesses.
- In the **second paragraph**, Gutenberg prints semicolons where the 1816 copy uses commas. Interpretations of the *author's* semicolon would fail the textual-witness test.
- Flaubert's **'Il se leva; sa casquette tomba'** shares a semicolon sign but presents two brisk physical events.
- The punctuation type alone does not determine temporal effect, syntactic relation, viewpoint, irony or artistry. Entire neighbouring clause structures matter.

This is a factual caution against the thin method "find semicolons → label technique". It does **not** say punctuation cannot be extraordinarily consequential.

## Contrast 3 — Dense material can be descriptive, syntactic, or acoustic

- Austen's opening delays a tensed verb behind layers of attributes, then complicates evaluation through small predicates and conjunctions.
- Flaubert's cap is decomposed into material shapes and parts, then the action contracts into very short events.
- His extended crowd-noise sentence describes rising, rolling, fading and returning, including a term from musical dynamics, **crescendo**.
- The appearance of sound words in the text is observable. Whether the phonetics of the entire French sentence recreate the disturbance is an unverified stronger claim.

**Not an equivalence claim:** a cap description and an opening assessment need not be alternative solutions to the same narrative problem. Their comparison is for perceptual variety, not to invent a grand opposition.

## Contrast 4 — Nonlocal effects without grand-narrative justification

- In *Emma*, the later disclosure of independence from Miss Taylor's authority modifies the earlier seemingly favourable portrait. This is a cross-paragraph relationship readable from exact phrasing.
- In *Bovary*, earlier public labelling as *nouveau* and later pronunciation of a name interact with the entire social situation; one word is carried into collective repeated sound.
- Neither observation requires settling whether each novel succeeds as an artistic whole.
- A proposed distant echo must show its later locus explicitly; do not invent work-wide motifs from a local repeated consonant.

## Direct disagreement among capable analysts is also part of evidence

Barbara Lively treats *seemed* as a crucial interpretive gate and explores the narrative's free indirect perspective. Tony Tanner's attention to Flaubert's substantivised *nouveau* similarly begins with a small trace, but travels toward a more abstract argument about newness. Their work involves different novels and **is not a disagreement**; it shows how interpretive readings can expand from real linguistic features. Separate the verified root from the critic's speculative reach.

## Negative control: counterfeit interpretation

The earlier STENDHAL library opening called independent changes in visits and loans a "contradiction". It manufactured a premise to support rhetorical effect. That is a **logical error before it is an aesthetic defect**.

By contrast, the opening of *Emma* gives a qualified valuation; later evidence revises how we take it. The Flaubert scene gives a name that changes audibly/graphically through its social reception. These contain precise textual events; don't praise them merely because they sound writerly, but don't mistake those events for rhetorical labels either.

## We have not established

- an objective technique count;
- that all annotations are independent operations;
- what reader populations actually infer or feel;
- author's intention at each comma, including those introduced by later editors;
- what mathematical representation a future engine needs.

## Next sampling route

For each opening, use at least one *unrelated* accomplished critic offering a genuinely different reading **of the same linguistic place**, not a general opinion on the book. In Flaubert's case, seek manuscript/edition evidence of how the cap passage changed. For Austen, seek textual-critical witnesses and a critic challenging Lively's emphasis on narrator/character viewpoint. Extend beyond prestigious novel openings to dull yet skilful scientific prose, children's writing, comedy and vivid popular journalism.
