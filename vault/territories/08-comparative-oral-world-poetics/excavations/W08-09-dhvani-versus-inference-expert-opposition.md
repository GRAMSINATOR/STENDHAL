## The rare, actually qualified opposition we want

Primary poem: Prakrit stanza beginning `bhama dhammia vīsattho`, identified in *Dhvanyāloka* 1.4 (the dog is killed by the lion, so the ascetic is encouraged to wander). The direct poetic utterance and the context reconstructed in later commentary are **separate source layers**.

- **Ānandavardhana, *Dhvanyāloka***: cites verse to demonstrate that the positive or reassuring literal utterance can give rise to an opposed, implicitly prohibitive meaning. His broader project distinguishes `dhvani` (suggestion) from ordinary direct and secondary meanings.
- **Mahimabhaṭṭa, *Vyaktiviveka***: contests whether a *special* power of poetic suggestion is needed at all; the apparent further meaning is obtained by `anumāna`, inference, using shared world knowledge.

A major secondary source closely documents the distinction and explicitly walks through the **same poem**:

Chettiarthodi Rajendran, “Poetry and Logic: Mahimabhaṭṭa's Epistemology,” *Parispanda*, 2024, https://parispanda.ssus.ac.in/index.php/2024/08/06/poetry/ . The author cites the primary *Vyaktiviveka* edition (R. P. Dwivedi, Varanasi 1982), with original passage/page references in notes.

Independent university monograph overview: James D. Reich, *To Savor the Meaning* (2021), ch. 4, “Mahimabhaṭṭa on Literary Knowing,” Oxford chapter abstract https://academic.oup.com/book/39365/chapter-abstract/339030752 . ## Original explanation, made concrete

The speaker tells the ascetic that his old threat (dog) has been eliminated by a fiercer animal (lion). An inference account reconstructs the bridge:

1. Ascetic is afraid of the dog.
2. Lion is more dangerous than dog, according to contextual worldly knowledge.
3. A still fiercer animal's presence makes entry undesirable.
4. The superficially permissive utterance therefore discourages the act.

The *dhvani* account, by contrast, does not deny that literal words, shared knowledge and context matter. Its claim is that a *further suggestive poetic function* is necessary to account for such nonliteral understanding. What counts as necessity, distinctness and apprehension is precisely disputed.

**No imaginary adjudication:** The supplied material does not settle whether a distinct mechanism is required. It establishes a genuine historically situated opposition about the explanation of an observed literary effect.

## Second case: experts interpret the *same utterance* differently

Rajendran also recounts interpretive divergence over a second *Dhvanyāloka* poem, translated as a heroine telling a formerly intimate man to go away and let her suffer the sighs and tears. The detailed original Sanskrit, original interpretive commentary and edition have **not** been independently collated here.

As reported by Rajendran:
- An interpretation associated with **Abhinavagupta** treats the man's current visit as merely courteous and hence hypocritical.
- **Mahimabhaṭṭa** reads the utterance as evidence of the man's affection for another woman, with sighs and tears standing as signs of separation/attachment.

The exact context limits inference. These are two differently attributed **reader-expert hypotheses**, neither proven as the sole recoverable authorial intention. Do not convert them into a tidy binary of “surface versus depth”: both interpreters read beyond literal instruction.

## Author's own critical friction

Rajendran also notes that Mahimabhaṭṭa reworks the `loka / veda / adhyātma` evidentiary framework from earlier dramatic scholarship to serve his inferential account and questions how securely the borrowed passage supports that new purpose. This is **scholarship critically auditing the epistemic grounds of the theorist**, not only reproducing his self-description.

One short primary Sanskrit string quoted by Rajendran (citing *Vyaktiviveka* p. 27): `sarva eva hi śabdo vyavahāraḥ … prāyeṇānumānarūpo ’vagantavyaḥ`. Rajendran reads this as the thesis that inferential structure pervades ordinary linguistic transactions as well as poetry. **Use the edition to verify completeness and exact segmentation before direct primary quotation in publication.**

## What this means for research design (not a STENDHAL rule)

At least three independent evaluation questions become visible:
1. **Result:** what does the interpreter say the utterance leads the hearer to understand?
2. **Evidence:** which exact words, facts, context, physical details, lexical relations, and cultural premises support it?
3. **Proposed cognition:** what process is said to explain the understanding—and how could it be distinguished from another mechanism producing the same verbal answer?

A textual output alone **does not expose internal cognition**. Never accept the model's polished after-the-fact “reason for my interpretation” as proof that it used that process when generating. To test mechanism, use controlled perturbations, response-time or human-reader evidence, mechanistic probes where ethically/technically possible, and record uncertainty.

## Negative cases and limits

- The lion example may not prove a unique interpretation without contextual knowledge; addressee may react differently.
- If everyone agrees on an implied caution, that does not prove agreement on its mechanism.
- If experts disagree on the lover's motive, that does not prove the literary work fails. The ambiguity may be functionally valuable, or their contexts may differ.
- Unlike a source-attested disagreement, our own proposed bridge to modern LLMs is entirely conjectural.
