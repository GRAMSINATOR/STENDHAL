# W08-07 — Ānandavardhana: an invitation that warns

**Parallel stream 02; 2026-10-12.** **Evidence:** C2 for the primary *Dhvanyāloka* transliterated edition's Gāthā 2.75 and commentary at 1.4 (GRETIL/TextGrid), plus selected independent scholarly discussion. Modern translation and contextual character motives need separate attributions.

The **quoted stanza is in Prakrit**, not Sanskrit. The edition directly follows it with a **Sanskrit rendering** in brackets, and associates it with *Gāthā* 2.75. Scholarly gloss: Saam Trivedi, “Anandavardhana on Literary Suggestion,” *Journal of Comparative Literature and Aesthetics* 48(4), Winter 2025, pp. 1–11, esp. 3–4: https://jcla.in/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/JCLA-48.4_Winter-2025_Saam-Trivedi.pdf .

Small Prakrit anchor (a portion of the verse, **not** an invented Sanskrit original):
`bhama dhammia vīsattho so suṇao ajja mārio deṇa`
Then: `golā-ṇaī-kaccha-kuḍaṅga-bāsiṇā daria-sīheṇa`.

**Our provisional paraphrase:** The speaker encourages a religious man to roam safely now that a dog has been killed by a fierce lion living in a nearby riverside thicket.

## Literal action versus socially situated outcome

The literal grammatical action is **permission/encouragement**. Yet the information that a **lion** has killed the frightening dog gives the addressee a reason *not to go near the thicket*. The *Dhvanyāloka* explicitly positions this as a case where what is expressed has an affirmative form while what is suggested can be prohibitive.

A key contextual qualification: the compressed Prakrit verse on its own does **not** explicitly recount the woman's amorous rendezvous. That fuller story appears in interpretive commentarial/modern scholarly reconstructions. In Trivedi's 2025 account, the female speaker wishes to keep the river grove available for meeting her beloved. Do not misattribute that narrative to every word of the cited two-line stanza itself.

## What is actually being enacted?

1. A superficially reassuring **speech act** and a threatening narrated fact coexist.
2. The listener must integrate **contextual hazard knowledge**, not just resolve lexical ambiguity. Lion > dog in danger is historically/culturally plausible, but the addressee's actual psychology is inferred rather than observed.
3. The utterance has at least two relevant **audiences**: its fictional interlocutor and the reader/hearer of the poem who may reconstruct the speaker's aim.
4. A literal paraphrase can be perfectly accurate at the clause level and still fail to record the **practical communicative act**.
5. Equating suggestion with sarcasm or lying would be an ungrounded simplification; there is no automatic formula for reversing an affirmative sentence.

## Source-near countercase

The immediately following example (same edited locus, *Dhvanyāloka* 1.4, a Prakrit verse beginning `attā ettha ṇimajjai`) is described as the reverse: literal prohibition can be a situational **invitation**. The first example thus does not claim that imperatives are always suspect; their apparent force interacts with material information and situation.

## Contrast with al-Jurjānī

[W08-06](W08-06-al-jurjani-negation-and-agent-focus.md) investigates whether changing Arabic constituent ordering under a negative affects information considered already established. Here the background facts conveyed literally can remain true while the intended **action** is contrary to the surface invitation. These are not one “subtext” operator.

## Proposed tests

- Give readers a sentence plus varied environmental context; assess whether they identify a practical invitation, warning or irony without relying only on grammatical mood.
- Present versions with the lion fact moved, removed, weakened, or made geographically remote; locate the point at which the inferred warning stops.
- Do not claim modern participant ratings establish the historic Prakrit author's exact intentions. Preserve reader pluralities and expert philology.

## Remaining work

Read original Prakrit with a specialist and compare edited witnesses; verify the *Gāthāsaptaśatī* tradition and different commentarial stories. Need primary original-language adversaries such as Mahimabhaṭṭa before declaring the controversy around *dhvani* settled. Do not use a generalised English translation as evidence for Prakrit acoustic craft.
